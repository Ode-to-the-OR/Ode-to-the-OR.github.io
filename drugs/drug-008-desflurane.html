Drug Deep Dive · Volatile Anaesthetic · No. 008

Desflurane

The fastest volatile agent to titrate in either direction — and, increasingly, the one whose place in the cupboard is being decided as much by regulation as by pharmacology.

Desflurane occupies a genuinely unusual position among the volatile agents: pharmacologically it is arguably the most controllable of the halogenated anaesthetics, with the fastest onset and offset of any potent inhalational agent in routine use — and yet its clinical footprint has been shrinking, not through any newly discovered safety concern, but because of its environmental impact. It requires a dedicated heated vaporiser owing to a boiling point close to room temperature, it is too pungent for inhalational induction, and it has recently gone from a mainstream default in some settings to a restricted or decommissioned agent in others. All of that is worth understanding together, not as separate trivia. Here is what matters about desflurane, from the physical chemistry up to the current regulatory picture.

1. Mechanism of Action

Desflurane is a fluorinated methyl ethyl ether, structurally very similar to isoflurane (differing by a single fluorine substitution in place of a chlorine atom) but with markedly different physical properties as a direct consequence of that substitution.

Where It Acts, and Why

GABA-A receptor potentiation and related ion channels: like the other modern volatile agents, desflurane produces general anaesthesia through a combination of GABA-A receptor potentiation, glycine receptor effects contributing to spinally-mediated immobility, and background potassium channel activation — the same broad multi-receptor mechanism shared across the halogenated ethers, with anaesthetic potency correlating with lipid solubility (the Meyer-Overton relationship) as much as with any single receptor-binding profile.

Physical chemistry, not receptor pharmacology, defines desflurane's clinical identity: desflurane's single fluorine-for-chlorine substitution relative to isoflurane dramatically lowers its boiling point (~23°C, versus isoflurane's ~48°C) and its blood:gas solubility (~0.42, the lowest of the halogenated agents in clinical use, comparable to nitrous oxide). These two physical properties — not any difference in receptor mechanism — are responsible for almost everything that makes desflurane clinically distinctive: its exceptionally fast titratability, its requirement for a specialised vaporiser, and its airway pungency.

Why a Special Vaporiser Is Required

Desflurane's boiling point of roughly 22.8–23.5°C sits close enough to typical ambient room temperature that, in a conventional variable-bypass vaporiser, it would boil unpredictably depending on the theatre environment, producing dangerously inconsistent and potentially massive overdosing. To deliver desflurane safely, a dedicated electrically heated and pressurised vaporiser (maintaining the liquid at approximately 39°C and roughly two atmospheres) injects a precisely metered vapour directly into the fresh gas flow, independent of ambient temperature — a genuinely different delivery mechanism from the variable-bypass vaporisers used for sevoflurane or isoflurane.

2. Pharmacokinetics

Desflurane's pharmacokinetics are dominated by its exceptionally low blood:gas solubility, which is what produces its signature speed of onset and, more clinically important, its speed of offset — a genuine advantage for rapid emergence and precise titration of depth throughout a case.

3. Physiological Effects, System by System

Cardiovascular

Produces dose-dependent reduction in systemic vascular resistance and mild-to-moderate blood pressure reduction, broadly similar to isoflurane. A distinctive feature: rapid increases in inspired desflurane concentration can transiently activate the sympathetic nervous system, producing tachycardia, hypertension, and elevated catecholamine levels — most pronounced when concentration is increased quickly, particularly above roughly 1 MAC, and more marked in smokers and younger patients. This effect is specific to the rate of concentration increase rather than the absolute concentration itself, and is generally attenuated by gradual titration or pre-treatment with opioids, beta-blockers, or alpha-2 agonists.

Respiratory

Produces dose-dependent respiratory depression similar in overall pattern to the other volatile agents. Desflurane is notably pungent — it reliably irritates the airway at concentrations sufficient for induction, provoking coughing, breath-holding, laryngospasm, and salivation — which is precisely why it is not used for inhalational induction and is instead introduced only after the airway is already secured via an IV agent, typically once an endotracheal tube or supraglottic device is in place.

Central Nervous System

Reduces cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption in a broadly dose-dependent fashion, with cerebral vasodilation and potential intracranial pressure effects at higher MAC values analogous to the other volatile agents — relevant to careful titration in neuroanaesthetic cases. Its fast offset is a genuine advantage where rapid neurological assessment after surgery is a priority, a consideration that has made it a historically favoured agent in some neurosurgical and bariatric practice specifically for this reason.

Neuromuscular

Produces dose-dependent skeletal muscle relaxation and potentiates non-depolarising neuromuscular blocking agents, consistent with the volatile agents as a class.

Other

Minimal hepatic metabolism translates to a very low theoretical burden of metabolite-related toxicity. Does trigger malignant hyperthermia in susceptible individuals, the same absolute contraindication that applies across the halogenated volatile agents as a class.

4. Clinical Uses

Desflurane's combination of exceptionally fast titratability and rapid emergence made it a strong choice in specific clinical scenarios, even as its overall use has declined for the environmental reasons discussed below.

Maintenance of General Anaesthesia (Post-Induction)

Used for maintenance once the airway is secured by another agent, never for inhalational induction, owing to its pungency.

Bariatric and Obesity Anaesthesia

Rapid offset even after prolonged cases has historically made desflurane a favoured choice in this population, where faster emergence and reduced context-sensitive accumulation are genuinely valuable.

Long or Complex Cases Where Fast Emergence Matters

Minimal accumulation with prolonged administration (low context-sensitive effect) supports predictable emergence even after extended anaesthesia duration.

Neuroanaesthesia (Selected Cases)

Fast offset supports prompt post-operative neurological assessment, a genuine historical rationale for its use in some neurosurgical practice.

Cases Requiring Precise, Rapid Titration of Depth

Low blood solubility allows anaesthetic depth to be adjusted and to take effect more quickly than with a more soluble agent — useful where rapid changes in depth are anticipated.

Day-Case / Ambulatory Anaesthesia (Where Still in Use)

Historically valued for supporting faster recovery room throughput, though this advantage over sevoflurane is generally modest in practice and is now weighed against environmental considerations in many centres.

5. Dosing & MAC

As with all volatile agents, desflurane "dosing" is expressed as inspired or end-tidal concentration relative to MAC rather than as a weight-based milligram dose.

Figures are illustrative and drawn from commonly cited ranges in the anaesthesia literature. Always confirm against your institution's protocol, vaporiser calibration, and current product labelling — MAC and dosing figures here are not a substitute for local guidelines or individualised titration.

Introduce Gradually, Not as a Step Change

Because rapid increases in inspired desflurane concentration can transiently activate the sympathetic nervous system, increasing the concentration gradually — rather than dialling straight to a target percentage — meaningfully reduces the tachycardia and hypertension this can otherwise provoke, particularly above roughly 1 MAC. This is a genuinely desflurane-specific consideration, more pronounced than with sevoflurane or isoflurane, and worth building into routine practice rather than treating as a rare edge case.

6. Cautions, Contraindications & Interactions

Absolute Contraindications

  • Known or suspected malignant hyperthermia susceptibility — desflurane is a recognised MH trigger agent, addressed further below
  • Known hypersensitivity to desflurane or other halogenated agents
  • Inhalational induction — not a true contraindication in the safety sense, but functionally excluded by its pungency; attempting mask induction with desflurane reliably provokes airway irritation and should not be attempted

Use With Caution

  • Coronary artery disease or conditions poorly tolerant of tachycardia — the sympathetic activation with rapid concentration increases is a genuine consideration in this population
  • Significantly reduced intracranial compliance — titrate carefully as with other volatile agents given dose-dependent cerebral vasodilation at higher MAC values
  • Reactive airway disease — desflurane's pungency can provoke airway irritation and bronchospasm more readily than sevoflurane, even once the airway is secured, particularly with rapid concentration increases

Interactions

  • Opioids and other sedative-hypnotics: significant MAC-sparing effect, as with the other volatile agents
  • Nitrous oxide: MAC-additive
  • Neuromuscular blocking agents: potentiated by desflurane
  • Beta-blockers, opioids, alpha-2 agonists: pre-treatment with these agents attenuates the sympathetic activation associated with rapid desflurane concentration increases

Malignant Hyperthermia and Desiccated Absorbent Carbon Monoxide Formation

Like other volatile agents, desflurane is a recognised MH trigger in susceptible individuals; management follows the same current MHAUS protocol described for other volatile agents — stop the trigger, hyperventilate on 100% oxygen via a non-triggering circuit, give dantrolene promptly, and manage the metabolic derangements that follow. Separately, desflurane (along with isoflurane and enflurane) can be degraded by significantly desiccated CO2 absorbent containing strong base (particularly older absorbents like Baralyme, and to a lesser extent soda lime containing KOH/NaOH) to form carbon monoxide — a distinct chemical reaction from sevoflurane's Compound A pathway, and one that specifically requires desiccated (dried-out) absorbent rather than normally hydrated absorbent to occur to a clinically relevant degree. This is why fresh gas flow should never be left running through a circuit over a break between cases (which dries out the absorbent), and why unusually rapid discoloration or unexpectedly high absorbent temperature should prompt absorbent replacement. Modern absorbents formulated without strong base (KOH/NaOH-free) substantially reduce or eliminate this risk regardless of desiccation state.

7. The Environmental Question

Desflurane's environmental impact has become one of the more actively debated and rapidly evolving aspects of anaesthetic practice, and the numbers involved are worth knowing precisely rather than in vague terms.

Desflurane is exhaled almost entirely unchanged and acts as a potent greenhouse gas once released into the atmosphere. Comparative analyses have put its global warming potential meaningfully higher than sevoflurane's — a full 240 mL bottle of desflurane has been estimated at roughly 886 kg of CO2-equivalent emissions, compared with roughly 49 kg CO2-equivalent for a 250 mL bottle of sevoflurane, a difference driven both by desflurane's higher intrinsic global warming potential per molecule and by its considerably lower potency (roughly one-third that of sevoflurane), meaning a larger volume must be administered to achieve an equivalent anaesthetic depth. Different published estimates place desflurane's overall climate impact per case at somewhere in the range of twenty to fifty times that of sevoflurane, depending on the specific methodology and clinical conditions modelled.

This has translated into concrete regulatory and institutional change rather than remaining a purely academic debate. The UK's NHS decommissioned routine desflurane use in 2024, and European Union F-gas regulation restricts desflurane use from January 2026 onward, permitting it only where no other anaesthetic can be used on medical grounds. The World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists has issued a statement prioritising lower-global-warming-potential volatile agents such as sevoflurane over desflurane where clinically equivalent options exist. Many institutions worldwide have independently reduced or eliminated routine desflurane use as part of broader sustainable anaesthesia initiatives, with documented reductions in institutional greenhouse gas emissions as a direct result.

A Genuine Practice Shift, Not a Fringe Concern

This is not a marginal environmental talking point — it represents an active, ongoing shift in mainstream anaesthetic practice, with real regulatory teeth in some jurisdictions already, and is likely to keep evolving. Whatever the local availability and institutional stance where you practise, it is worth knowing the current regulatory and society-level guidance in your own setting specifically, since this is genuinely a moving target across different countries and healthcare systems at present.

8. Paediatric Considerations

Desflurane's role in paediatric anaesthesia is narrower than sevoflurane's, constrained directly by the same pungency that rules it out of adult inhalational induction.

  • Not used for inhalational induction in children for the same reason it is avoided in adults — airway irritation, coughing, and a significantly increased risk of laryngospasm at induction-relevant concentrations.
  • Can be used for maintenance once the airway is secured via IV induction or after mask induction with a non-pungent agent such as sevoflurane, analogous to its adult use pattern.
  • Emergence agitation has been reported with desflurane in children, similarly to sevoflurane, though the comparative incidence and relevant risk factors are less extensively characterised than for sevoflurane specifically.
  • Given desflurane's overall declining use and the specific pungency-related restriction on induction, sevoflurane remains the dominant agent in most paediatric anaesthetic practice, with desflurane reserved for specific maintenance scenarios where its titratability offers a genuine advantage.

9. What Actually Happens at the Bedside

The textbook version of desflurane use is a vaporiser that responds almost instantly to dial changes and a patient who wakes up fast and clear-headed at the end of the case. The bedside version has more texture than that.

The first thing worth genuine attention is that the speed advantage desflurane offers on emergence can tempt a rushed concentration increase mid-case when a sudden increase in surgical stimulus demands deeper anaesthesia quickly. Stepping the dial straight up rather than titrating gradually is exactly the scenario most likely to provoke the transient tachycardia and hypertension associated with rapid concentration increases — the drug's fast responsiveness is a genuine strength, but it rewards deliberate, incremental titration rather than large abrupt adjustments, even when the clinical situation feels urgent.

The second thing is that the vaporiser itself is a piece of equipment worth actually understanding rather than treating as an interchangeable box. A desflurane vaporiser that has been recently moved, is warming up from a cold start, or has been used inconsistently can behave differently from one that has been running steadily — checking that the vaporiser has reached and is maintaining its operating temperature before relying on it for a time-critical induction sequence is a genuine, practical habit worth having, not an excessive precaution.

The third thing is that the environmental conversation is increasingly a genuine part of the clinical decision, not a separate administrative concern to be handled elsewhere. Choosing sevoflurane over desflurane where either would serve the case equally well is now, in many settings, treated as an active clinical decision with its own rationale — worth being able to explain and apply consistently, rather than defaulting to whichever vaporiser happens to be plugged in that day out of habit.

10. Pearls & Pitfalls

The Core Takeaway

Desflurane's pharmacology is genuinely excellent by several measures — fastest titratability, lowest metabolic burden, most predictable emergence even after long cases. Its declining use reflects an external factor (environmental impact) rather than any newly discovered clinical shortcoming, and understanding that distinction matters: this is not a drug that has become less safe, but one whose overall footprint has become harder to justify where an equally effective, lower-impact alternative exists.

The Honest Bit

Desflurane is, by most narrow clinical measures, an excellent drug — arguably the best-titrating volatile agent available. The environmental case against it is real, well-quantified, and has already produced concrete regulatory change in some jurisdictions, and that is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as an abstraction. At the same time, in the specific clinical scenarios where its properties offer a genuine, meaningful advantage — certain bariatric, prolonged, or precision-titration cases — it remains a legitimate clinical choice where locally available and permitted, and that legitimacy shouldn't be lost in a blanket "never use this" framing either. Both things are true at once, and the honest position holds both.

  • Pitfall: Increasing desflurane concentration as a rapid step change in response to a sudden stimulus, rather than titrating gradually to avoid the sympathetic activation this can provoke.
  • Pitfall: Attempting or considering desflurane for inhalational induction — its pungency makes this unsuitable regardless of the patient or clinical scenario.
  • Pearl: The fast offset genuinely matters in prolonged cases and specific populations (bariatric, some neurosurgical) — know when this advantage is clinically meaningful rather than simply a matter of habit or convenience.
  • Pearl: Never leave fresh gas flow running unattended through a circuit between cases — desiccated absorbent is the specific precondition for clinically relevant carbon monoxide formation with this drug.
  • Pitfall: Treating the environmental impact conversation as outside the scope of clinical decision-making — in many current settings, it is now a genuine, active part of the choice between otherwise clinically equivalent agents.
The best-titrating volatile agent in the cupboard is currently being weighed against a global-warming-potential figure most other drugs never have to answer to. Both facts are true, and neither cancels the other out. — A closing thought
