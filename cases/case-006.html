The Balloon and the Right Heart: When PBMV Fails in Severe Mitral Stenosis

A 48-year-old woman with severe mitral stenosis and severe pulmonary hypertension undergoes percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty — and returns with severe mitral regurgitation, pulmonary edema, and a right heart under siege.

Cardiac Mitral Stenosis PBMV Pulmonary Hypertension MVR CPB

A 48-year-old woman presented with three months of exertional breathlessness that resolved with rest. She had no chest pain, no palpitations, no orthopnoea, no oedema. She was not hypertensive, not diabetic, had no history of stroke or coronary disease. She had never smoked. Her ECG showed T-wave inversions in leads III, aVF, and V2–V6. Her echocardiogram told a different story: severe mitral stenosis, severe pulmonary hypertension, and a right heart under pressure.

The plan was percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV). The balloon crossed the valve, dilated it — and something went wrong. The V wave shot up. Severe mitral regurgitation. Pulmonary oedema. The patient was shifted for emergency mitral valve replacement (MVR). This case is about the right heart, the pulmonary circulation, and the anaesthetic battle to keep both alive.

ECG Findings

The ECG showed T-wave inversions in leads III, aVF, and V2–V6. These are non-specific but may reflect:

  • Right heart strain: T-wave inversions in inferior leads (III, aVF) and precordial leads (V2–V6) can indicate RV pressure overload and strain — consistent with severe pulmonary hypertension.
  • LV diastolic overload: T-wave changes in V4–V6 can reflect altered LV filling dynamics in severe MS with reduced preload.
  • Chronic LA hypertension: Repolarisation abnormalities are common in chronic mitral valve disease.

There were no ischaemic changes, no arrhythmias, and no evidence of acute coronary syndrome. The ECG was consistent with the underlying valvular and pulmonary vascular disease.

The 2D echo missed the LAA clot

The 2D transthoracic echo reported "no clot." The pre-procedure TEE revealed organic clot in the left atrial appendage (LAA) with spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) and reduced LAA emptying velocity (38 cm/s). This is a critical teaching point: TEE is far more sensitive than TTE for LAA thrombus detection. In patients with severe MS, LA dilation, and SEC, TEE is mandatory before PBMV.

Wilkins score 9/16 — borderline

A score of 9 is above the ideal threshold of 8. The high subvalvular score (3) and morphology score (3) indicate significant leaflet and subvalvular disease. This predicts a higher risk of suboptimal PBMV result and a greater likelihood of complications, including leaflet tear and severe MR. The presence of LAA clot further complicates the decision.

Pathophysiology of Severe Mitral Stenosis

Severe MS is not just a valve problem — it is a haemodynamic cascade that affects the left atrium, pulmonary circulation, right heart, and eventually the left ventricle.

→
Obstruction at the mitral valve
Restricted opening of the mitral valve creates a pressure gradient between the left atrium and left ventricle. Normal MVA is 4–6 cm²; this patient's MVA was 1.2–1.4 cm².
→
Left atrial hypertension
Elevated LA pressure (mean 24 mmHg pre-PBMV) leads to LA dilation, increased wall stress, and a predisposition to atrial fibrillation.
→
Pulmonary venous congestion
Backward transmission of pressure into the pulmonary veins causes pulmonary venous and capillary hypertension, leading to interstitial and alveolar oedema, reduced lung compliance, and increased work of breathing.
→
Reactive pulmonary hypertension
Chronic pulmonary venous hypertension triggers vasoconstriction and structural remodelling of the pulmonary arterioles. This patient had severe PAH with RVSP ~70–100 mmHg.
→
Right ventricular pressure overload
The RV must generate higher pressures to overcome pulmonary vascular resistance. This leads to RV hypertrophy, dilation, and eventually dysfunction.
→
Septal shift and D-shaped LV
RV dilation pushes the interventricular septum leftward, compressing the LV cavity. The LV becomes D-shaped, impairing diastolic filling and reducing stroke volume.
→
Reduced LV preload and cardiac output
The combination of mitral obstruction, septal shift, and reduced LV compliance leads to low LV end-diastolic volume and low cardiac output. The patient becomes dependent on adequate preload and sinus rhythm.
→
LAA stasis and thrombus formation
LA dilation and low LAA emptying velocity (38 cm/s) predispose to blood stasis, spontaneous echo contrast, and thrombus formation — as seen in this patient.

The right heart is the problem

In severe MS with severe PAH, the right ventricle is the vulnerable chamber. It is pressure-loaded, dilated, and poorly tolerant of any additional afterload increase. Anaesthetic management must focus on protecting the RV: maintaining systemic vascular tone, avoiding hypoxia and hypercarbia, preserving sinus rhythm, and using pulmonary vasodilators and inotropes judiciously.

Pulmonary Hypertension and the Right Heart

Severe pulmonary hypertension (RVSP ~70–100 mmHg) in this patient was not a passive consequence — it was an active driver of morbidity and the central challenge for anaesthetic management.

Why the right heart fails under pressure

  • The RV is a volume pump, not a pressure pump: The thin-walled RV is designed to eject into a low-resistance pulmonary circuit. When PVR rises, the RV must generate much higher pressures to maintain forward flow.
  • RV ischaemia: RV hypertrophy increases oxygen demand, while systemic hypotension reduces coronary perfusion to the RV. This supply–demand mismatch can precipitate RV failure.
  • Interventricular dependence: RV dilation shifts the septum leftward, impairing LV filling and reducing cardiac output. This creates a vicious cycle: RV failure → LV failure → systemic hypotension → RV ischaemia.
  • Pulmonary hypertensive crisis: Any stimulus that acutely increases PVR — hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis, pain, light anaesthesia, hypothermia — can trigger a catastrophic rise in pulmonary pressures, leading to RV failure and cardiovascular collapse.

The D-shaped LV

The D-shaped LV on TEE is a direct sign of RV pressure overload. It means the RV is pushing the septum into the LV cavity, reducing LV filling. This is a red flag for severe RV strain and predicts difficult haemodynamic management, especially during induction and CPB weaning.

The PBMV Procedure — What Happened

Percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) is a catheter-based procedure that dilates the stenotic mitral valve using a balloon. The standard technique is the Inoue balloon, which is advanced from the femoral vein, across the interatrial septum, into the left atrium, and then across the mitral valve into the left ventricle.

Step 1 — Femoral venous access
The right femoral vein is cannulated, and a guidewire is advanced into the right atrium.
Step 2 — Transseptal puncture
A needle is advanced through the interatrial septum to enter the left atrium. This is a critical step — it must be performed under TEE or fluoroscopic guidance.
Step 3 — Balloon advancement
The Inoue balloon is advanced over a wire across the mitral valve into the left ventricle. The distal portion of the balloon is inflated first, then the proximal portion — this "stepwise" inflation helps anchor the balloon across the valve.
Step 4 — Balloon inflation and deflation
The balloon is inflated to dilate the valve commissures. The goal is to split the fused commissures without causing leaflet tear or severe MR.
Step 5 — Assessment
Post-dilation, the valve area, gradient, and MR are assessed. In this patient, the post-PBMV V wave was ~80 mmHg, and echo showed severe MR.

The Complication

Post-PBMV, the patient developed severe mitral regurgitation. The V wave on left atrial pressure tracing rose to ~75–80 mmHg — a giant V wave indicating severe MR. The patient developed pulmonary oedema and was shifted to the ICU, then to the operating room for emergency MVR.

Why PBMV failed in this patient

Several factors predicted a poor outcome:

  • Wilkins score 9/16: Above the ideal threshold of 8, indicating significant leaflet and subvalvular disease.
  • Subvalvular grade 3: Severe subvalvular thickening makes commissural splitting difficult and increases the risk of leaflet tear.
  • LAA clot: A contraindication to PBMV due to embolic risk. The presence of organic clot and SEC indicates a high-risk atrium.
  • Severe PAH: The right heart was already under pressure; the acute volume overload from severe MR precipitated pulmonary oedema and RV failure.

The Decision to Proceed with MVR

Following the failed PBMV, the patient developed severe MR with pulmonary oedema. Medical management was insufficient. The decision was made to proceed with emergency mitral valve replacement (MVR) on cardiopulmonary bypass.

The anaesthetic plan needed to address:

  • Severe pulmonary hypertension with a dilated, pressure-loaded RV.
  • D-shaped LV with impaired filling and a small effective cavity.
  • Severe MR with pulmonary oedema and acute volume overload of the LA and pulmonary circulation.
  • Right heart failure risk during induction, CPB, and weaning.
  • Coagulopathy from CPB and the need for blood products.
  • Postoperative ventilation — the patient was not extubated at the end of the case.

Anaesthetic Management

Pre-induction planning

The patient was shifted to the cardiac operating room with continuous monitoring. The goals were:

  • Maintain systemic vascular tone to preserve coronary perfusion to the RV.
  • Avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, and acidosis — all increase PVR.
  • Maintain sinus rhythm or controlled ventricular rate. Tachycardia reduces diastolic filling time in MS; bradycardia reduces cardiac output.
  • Ensure adequate preload but avoid over-transfusion, which can worsen pulmonary oedema.
  • Have inotropes, vasopressors, and pulmonary vasodilators ready before induction.

Induction goals in severe PAH

The induction agent should provide haemodynamic stability without significant drops in SVR or increases in PVR. Options include etomidate (haemodynamically neutral), ketamine (sympathomimetic, but may increase PVR), or a high-dose opioid technique (fentanyl or remifentanil). Propofol should be used cautiously — it can cause significant vasodilation and hypotension.

Induction and airway management

The patient was induced with a carefully titrated technique. A large-bore endotracheal tube was placed, and the patient was ventilated with a lung-protective strategy: low tidal volumes, PEEP titrated to avoid derecruitment, and FiO₂ adjusted to maintain SpO₂ >92%. Hypercarbia was avoided — it increases PVR and can precipitate RV failure.

Intercostal nerve block (ICN)

An intercostal nerve block was performed for intraoperative and postoperative analgesia. This reduces opioid requirements, improves pain control, and helps prevent pulmonary complications. In a patient with severe PAH, adequate analgesia is critical — pain increases sympathetic tone, PVR, and myocardial oxygen demand.

Monitoring

  • Arterial line: Continuous blood pressure monitoring and ABG sampling.
  • Central venous catheter: CVP monitoring, vasopressor infusion, and fluid administration.
  • TEE: Essential for assessing valve function, MR severity, RV function, and guiding weaning from CPB.
  • Temperature monitoring: For CPB management.
  • Urine output: Hourly measurement.
  • ABG: Regular monitoring for oxygenation, ventilation, and acid–base status.

Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB) Management

Heparinisation
Heparin was administered to achieve an ACT >480 seconds. The post-heparin ACT was 540 seconds — adequate for CPB.
On-pump management
CPB was initiated. Dexmedetomidine was used during the on-pump period for sedation and haemodynamic stability. Dexmedetomidine reduces sympathetic tone, may lower PVR, and provides analgesia without respiratory depression.
Myocardial protection
Antegrade and/or retrograde cardioplegia was administered. Hypothermia was used as appropriate. The myocardium was protected during aortic cross-clamping.
MVR
The mitral valve was replaced. The surgeon assessed the valve and chose the appropriate prosthesis (mechanical or bioprosthetic).
Weaning from CPB
This was the critical moment. The RV had been unloaded during CPB, but now had to eject against a high-resistance pulmonary circulation. Inotropes and vasopressors were used to support the RV and maintain systemic pressure.
Protamine administration
After CPB, protamine was given to reverse heparin. The post-protamine ACT was 105 seconds — adequate reversal. Blood and FFP were administered as required.

Inotropes and Vasopressors Used

The patient required multiple agents before, during, and after CPB. The specific combination reflects the need to support both the right and left ventricles while maintaining systemic vascular tone.

Why milrinone matters in severe PAH

Milrinone is a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor that increases intracellular cAMP. It has two key effects in this context: inotropy (improves RV contractility) and pulmonary vasodilation (reduces PVR). This makes it an ideal agent for RV failure with pulmonary hypertension. However, it also causes systemic vasodilation, so it is often combined with noradrenaline to maintain systemic vascular tone.

Why the Patient Was Not Extubated

At the end of the procedure, the patient remained intubated and was shifted to the ICU with full inotropic and vasopressor support (noradrenaline, dopamine, adrenaline, and milrinone). This was a deliberate decision based on several factors:

Severe Pulmonary Hypertension

The pulmonary circulation remained highly reactive. Any stimulus — hypoxia, hypercarbia, pain, light sedation — could trigger a pulmonary hypertensive crisis. Controlled ventilation and sedation allowed for tight regulation of oxygenation and CO₂.

Right Ventricular Dysfunction

The RV was dilated and pressure-loaded. It needed inotropic support (milrinone, adrenaline) and careful preload management. Spontaneous ventilation would increase RV afterload and could precipitate failure.

Pulmonary Oedema

The patient had developed pulmonary oedema from acute severe MR. The lungs needed time to recover, and positive pressure ventilation helped maintain alveolar recruitment.

Post-CPB Stunning

Myocardial stunning after CPB is common. The heart needed time to recover, and inotropic support was essential to maintain cardiac output.

Coagulopathy

CPB causes dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy. The patient received blood and FFP. Extubation in the presence of coagulopathy and potential bleeding risk was not advisable.

Planned Re-exploration Risk

In complex cardiac surgery, the risk of bleeding or tamponade requiring re-exploration is significant. Keeping the patient intubated and sedated allowed for safe transfer and monitoring.

Complications and Pitfalls

Pulmonary Hypertensive Crisis

Any acute increase in PVR — hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis, pain, hypothermia — can precipitate a crisis. Management: 100% O₂, hyperventilation, milrinone, noradrenaline, and iNO if available.

RV Failure

The RV is the most vulnerable chamber. Maintain systemic vascular tone, avoid excessive preload, and use inotropes early. TEE monitoring is essential.

LV Failure After MVR

After MVR, the LV is suddenly exposed to increased preload. A previously "protected" LV may fail. Inotropic support and careful volume management are critical.

Bleeding and Coagulopathy

CPB causes coagulopathy. Monitor ACT, platelet count, and fibrinogen. Transfuse blood products as needed. Protamine reversal must be adequate.

Air Embolism

During MVR, air can enter the left heart. Meticulous de-airing is essential. TEE guidance helps confirm adequate de-airing before weaning from CPB.

Arrhythmias

Atrial fibrillation is common in MS. Post-MVR, sinus rhythm or controlled rate is important for LV filling. Amiodarone or pacing may be required.

Guidelines, Protocols & Evidence

Key guidelines

  • ACC/AHA (2020): PBMV is indicated for symptomatic severe MS with favourable valve morphology (Wilkins score ≤8) and no LAA thrombus. TEE is mandatory before the procedure to exclude LAA clot.
  • ESC/EACTS (2021): MVR is indicated for severe MR or failed PBMV with haemodynamic compromise. Perioperative TEE is essential for assessment and guidance.
  • ESAIC/ESTS (2022): In cardiac surgery with pulmonary hypertension, maintain systemic vascular tone, avoid increases in PVR, and use TEE for monitoring. Pulmonary vasodilators (milrinone, iNO) should be available.
  • STS/SCA (2021): Protective ventilation, careful fluid management, and early use of inotropes/vasopressors are recommended in cardiac surgery with RV dysfunction.

Stepwise anaesthetic protocol for MVR in severe PAH

Step 1 — Pre-operative optimisation
Assess PAH severity, RV function, and volume status. Review TEE for LAA clot, MR severity, and LV function. Ensure blood products are available. Plan for postoperative ventilation.
Step 2 — Monitoring and access
Arterial line, CVC, TEE, temperature probe, urinary catheter. Have inotropes and vasopressors ready before induction.
Step 3 — Induction
Slow, controlled induction with etomidate or high-dose opioid. Avoid hypotension and tachycardia. Maintain SVR.
Step 4 — Maintenance
Balanced anaesthesia with volatile (low MAC) or TIVA. Avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, and acidosis. Maintain sinus rhythm or controlled rate.
Step 5 — CPB and MVR
Heparinise (ACT >480s). Initiate CPB. Dexmedetomidine infusion. Myocardial protection. MVR. De-air carefully.
Step 6 — Weaning from CPB
Assess with TEE. Start milrinone and noradrenaline. Maintain systemic pressure. Avoid RV distension. Gradually wean from CPB.
Step 7 — Post-CPB management
Continue inotropes/vasopressors. Monitor ABG, ACT, and coagulation. Transfuse as needed. Transfer to ICU intubated.

What I Keep Coming Back To

This case is a study in right heart physiology and pulmonary vascular control. The left-sided valve was the trigger, but the right heart was the battlefield. The failed PBMV created a sudden, severe volume overload on an already compromised pulmonary circulation, and the anaesthetic team had to navigate the narrow path between systemic hypotension and pulmonary hypertensive crisis.

The decision to proceed with PBMV despite a Wilkins score of 9 and an LAA clot is a reminder that clinical decisions are rarely binary. The patient was highly symptomatic, and the team may have judged that the risks of intervention were outweighed by the risks of untreated severe MS. The complication — severe MR — is a known risk of PBMV, particularly in valves with subvalvular disease and borderline morphology.

The intraoperative management was a masterclass in multimodal haemodynamic support: noradrenaline to maintain systemic tone, milrinone for RV inotropy and pulmonary vasodilation, dopamine and adrenaline for additional support, dexmedetomidine for sedation, and meticulous coagulation management with heparin and protamine. The patient was not extubated — and that was the right call. The right heart needed time.

In severe mitral stenosis with pulmonary hypertension, the left atrium is the pressure chamber, the pulmonary circulation is the resistance circuit, and the right ventricle is the victim. Protect the right heart, and the rest follows. — What this case taught me
