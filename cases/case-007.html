Case 007 · Neuroanaesthesia · Neurocritical Care · Trauma

Pneumocephalus with Pneumoventricle: More Than Just Air

A 27-year-old man with a frontal sinus fracture, a broken skull base and air in his sulci and ventricles. This case is about traumatic pneumocephalus and how it shapes every airway, ventilation and ICU decision that follows.

Neuroanaesthesia Pneumocephalus TBI ICP Critical Care

After a road traffic accident, a 27-year-old man had a right frontal contusion, intraventricular blood, a small left temporal extradural haematoma and a comminuted fracture running through both walls of the frontal sinus, the orbit, the maxilla and the clivus. His GCS was 13, but the CT showed air in the sulci, cerebellar folia and ventricles. Air inside the dura means a dural breach, and it changes how the airway is secured, how the patient is ventilated, which gases are used, and how he is moved.

Initial NCCT Brain — Admission

Serial axial sections without contrast. Posterior cranial fossa including 4th ventricle normal.

Key findings

  • Right frontal lobe haemorrhagic contusion with surrounding vasogenic edema
  • Intraventricular haemorrhage in the right lateral ventricle
  • Pneumocephalus — air foci in bilateral cortical sulci and cerebellar folia
  • Pneumoventriculus — air in the supra- and infratentorial ventricular system
  • Extradural haemorrhage along the left temporal convexity, maximum thickness 8 mm
  • Comminuted displaced right frontal bone fracture extending into both walls of the frontal sinus (haemosinus)
  • Comminuted displaced left temporal bone fracture (squamous part) and greater wing of sphenoid
  • Clivus fracture extending to the posterior wall of the left sphenoid sinus
  • Right orbital floor and lamina papyracea fractures with periorbital edema and ecchymosis
  • Subgaleal emphysema in the right premaxillary and preseptal region

Impression: Haemorrhagic contusion in right frontal lobe. Intraventricular haemorrhage. Extradural haemorrhage along left temporal convexity. Pneumocephalus and pneumoventriculus. Multiple fractures as described.

Where the air lies

Location What it suggests
Epidural or subdural (frontal)Common after frontal sinus or anterior skull base fracture. Bilateral frontal subdural air that separates and points the frontal lobes is the Mount Fuji sign, a marker of tension.
Sulcal and cisternal (subarachnoid), cerebellar foliaAir has passed through the dura and arachnoid. Seen in this patient.
Intraventricular (pneumoventriculus)Air has reached the CSF pathways, again through a dural breach. Seen in this patient.
Confirming it is airAir measures about −1000 HU and is black on lung windows. Fat is about −100 HU, so a window change separates them.

Air seen on the first CT within hours of injury is acute pneumocephalus. Air that appears after about 72 hours is called delayed and often follows CSF leak or ventilation or drainage events.

Pearl — Pneumocephalus and the Trauma Airway

Pneumocephalus and pneumoventriculus after skull base fracture mandate avoidance of nitrous oxide — it diffuses into air-filled spaces and can expand intracranial air, raising ICP. Positive pressure ventilation through a disrupted dura requires vigilance for air leak and tension pneumocephalus.

Follow-up NCCT Brain — Interim and Day 10

Serial follow-up imaging was performed. An interim NCCT already demonstrated resolving bilateral frontal contusions with stable fracture configuration and no progression of the extradural haemorrhage.

The repeat NCCT on Day 10 confirmed the trend:

  • Resolving contusions in bilateral frontal lobes (R > L)
  • Subgaleal haematoma along the frontal convexity with air foci, maximum thickness 5 mm
  • Extradural haemorrhage along left temporal convexity reduced to 6 × 4 mm (from 8 mm)
  • Multiple fractures as previously described

Impression: Resolving contusions in bilateral frontal lobes. Subgaleal haematoma along the frontal convexity. Multiple fractures as described.

Serial imaging is not just about what has changed. It is about what has not changed — the fractures are stable, the EDH is smaller, the contusions are resolving. In neurotrauma, stability across multiple scans is the finding we hope for. — Case 007, serial imaging review

The Fundamental Dilemma: Brain vs. Lung

This case sits at the intersection of two competing physiological priorities. Every ventilator change, every fluid bolus, every position adjustment must be evaluated through both lenses.

🧠 Brain Protection

PaCO₂: Normocapnia (35–38 mmHg) — avoid hypercapnia (raises ICP) and hypocapnia (ischemia).

PEEP: May increase ICP by impeding cerebral venous drainage; use with caution and monitor ICP.

Head position: Head of bed 30° promotes cerebral venous drainage.

Sedation: Needed to control ICP, facilitate ventilation, and prevent coughing.

Fluids: Avoid hypotonic fluids (worsen cerebral edema); maintain euvolemia.

🫁 Lung Protection

PaCO₂: Permissive hypercapnia may be tolerated in ARDS.

PEEP: Recruits collapsed alveoli, improves oxygenation, reduces atelectrauma.

Head position: 30–45° reduces VAP risk but may worsen dependent lung atelectasis.

Sedation: Minimize to promote weaning and reduce VAP risk.

Fluids: Conservative fluids may worsen lung edema; balance against perfusion needs.

The evidence, as synthesised in recent narrative reviews, supports a physiology-driven, patient-specific approach that integrates lung mechanics, gas exchange, and cerebral hemodynamics — rather than protocolised strategies that prioritise one organ over the other.

Precautions: Airway to Transport

I — Airway
Oral rapid sequence induction (vomiting, full stomach, and later proven aspiration), controlled blood pressure and avoidance of a laryngoscopy pressor surge. No nasal route of any kind: nasotracheal tube, nasal airway, nasogastric tube. Use the orogastric route. Gentle, low-pressure mask ventilation if needed; manual in-line stabilisation until the spine is cleared (here the spine was normal on imaging).
II — Ventilation
Once the airway is secured with a cuffed tube, lung-protective ventilation is standard. Avoid coughing and bucking (adequate sedation, relaxant for suctioning if needed), avoid unnecessary high PEEP and peak pressures, and avoid blind nasopharyngeal suction. CPAP, BiPAP and high-flow nasal oxygen carry a theoretical risk of forcing air intracranially, which matters when planning weaning from the ventilator.
III — Gases
Avoid nitrous oxide. It is roughly 30 times more soluble than nitrogen, so it enters an air pocket faster than nitrogen leaves and can turn simple pneumocephalus into tension. Avoid it until the air has resolved on imaging. High FiO₂ lowers blood nitrogen and speeds absorption (supported by theory and small series, Dexter 1996), but it has to be balanced against oxygen toxicity and absorption atelectasis, especially with lung injury.
IV — CSF drainage and posture
Head up about 30° remains standard for ICP and CSF leak. Excess CSF drainage from an EVD or lumbar drain can draw air in (soda-bottle effect), so drain with care and avoid sitting upright with an open drain. Avoid straining, Valsalva and forceful nose-blowing once awake.
V — Transport and flight
CT trips need an attentive, stable transfer. For air transport, cabin pressure at about 8,000 ft is around 565 mmHg against 760 at sea level, so by Boyle's law trapped gas expands by about one-third.

ICP Monitoring: The Bolt

An intraparenchymal ICP monitor (bolt) was placed via a right frontal burr hole at Kocher's point, inserted to a depth of approximately 5 cm. This is the most common device for acute monitoring, providing continuous pressure readings without the technical challenges of ventriculostomy in compressed ventricles.

The patient's recorded ICP values — 12, 18, 10, 14 mmHg — remained below the treatment threshold. The transient elevation to 18 mmHg warranted vigilance but not escalation beyond conservative management.

Stepwise treatment of raised ICP

Step 1 — Basic measures
Head elevation 30°, neutral neck position, normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–38 mmHg), adequate analgesia and sedation.
Step 2 — Osmotherapy
Hypertonic saline (first-line) or mannitol (second-line). Monitor serum sodium and osmolality. Avoid hypotension during infusion.
Step 3 — Sedation and analgesia
Propofol and fentanyl, titrated to ICP and hemodynamics. Avoid hypotension.
Step 4 — Neuromuscular blockade
Short-term use for refractory ICP. Monitor with train-of-four.
Step 5 — Barbiturates
For refractory intracranial hypertension in hemodynamically stable patients. Requires EEG monitoring.
Step 6 — Surgical decompression
Decompressive craniectomy for refractory cases. Multidisciplinary decision.

Conservative Management of Frontal Contusions

Not every contusion needs an operation. In this patient, the right frontal haemorrhagic contusion and the 8 mm left temporal EDH were managed conservatively — a decision grounded in both imaging and clinical trajectory.

Rationale for conservative management

  • Contusion size and location: Frontal contusions are often "silent" — they may cause behavioural and cognitive changes rather than herniation. Without mass effect, midline shift, or cisternal effacement, surgery offers no clear benefit.
  • EDH thickness < 10 mm: The left temporal EDH measured 8 mm with no midline shift and no neurological deterioration. Guidelines support non-operative management for EDH < 10 mm with GCS > 8 and no focal deficit.
  • ICP monitoring: With an ICP bolt in situ and readings consistently below 20 mmHg, there was objective evidence that intracranial pressure was controlled.
  • Serial imaging: Both the interim follow-up and the Day 10 NCCT showed resolving contusions and a smaller EDH — confirming the conservative path was working across multiple time points, not just once.
  • Avoiding second-hit injury: Surgery in a polytrauma patient with aspiration pneumonitis and hypernatremia carries additional risk. Avoiding an unnecessary operation is itself a form of organ protection.

Pearl — When to operate on contusions

Surgery is indicated for contusions with progressive neurological deterioration, refractory intracranial hypertension, significant mass effect with midline shift, or cisternal compression. In the absence of these, conservative management with ICP monitoring and serial CT is the standard of care — and often the better path.

Ventilation Strategy: Walking the Tightrope

Initial ventilation — VC/AC

The patient was initially ventilated in volume-controlled (VC/AC) mode. This mode is widely used in neurocritical care because it guarantees a constant tidal volume and thus stable PaCO₂, which directly influences cerebral vessel diameter and ICP.

Transition to PRVC

The patient was later transitioned to Pressure-Regulated Volume Control (PRVC). This mode delivers a set tidal volume at the lowest possible pressure, automatically adjusting inspiratory pressure to changes in compliance and resistance.

Pearl — Why PRVC in TBI with aspiration pneumonitis?

PRVC provides a constant tidal volume (important for CO₂ stability and ICP control), a decelerating inspiratory flow pattern, and automatic adjustment for deteriorating lung compliance. In a patient with both brain injury and aspiration pneumonitis, this is a theoretical advantage: it maintains CO₂ stability while adapting to changing pulmonary mechanics.

Hypernatremia Management

Hypernatremia (serum Na⁺ > 145 mmol/L) is common in neurocritical care, affecting 6–47% of ICU patients, and is independently associated with mortality.

Causes in this patient

  • Reduced water intake — sedation, intubation, impaired consciousness
  • Increased water loss — vomiting, insensible losses, possible osmotic diuresis
  • Sodium administration — resuscitation fluids, medications

Management principles

  1. Correct volume deficits first — with isotonic fluids (0.9% NaCl) if hypovolemic.
  2. Then replace free water deficit — via enteral route (orogastric tube) with sterile water or IV D5W.
  3. Correction rate — not to exceed 10–12 mEq/L per 24 hours (chronic hypernatremia).
  4. Monitor serum sodium every 4–6 hours during active correction.
Free Water Deficit (L) = TBW × (Serum Na⁺ / 140 − 1)
TBW = body weight (kg) × 0.6 in young men

Distilled or sterile water

Suitable for enteral free-water flushes. Never intravenous on its own: it is profoundly hypotonic and causes haemolysis. For IV free water use 5% dextrose or 0.45% saline, noting hyperglycaemia and oedema risks. Resuscitate with isotonic fluid; avoid albumin in TBI (SAFE study).

In this patient, free water was replaced via the orogastric route alongside careful sodium monitoring. Enteral replacement is preferred when the gut is functional — it is simple, safe, and avoids the glucose load of IV D5W.

POCUS findings in aspiration pneumonitis

  • Subpleural consolidation — peripheral lung involvement
  • Dynamic air bronchogram — patent airways within consolidation; distinguishes from atelectasis
  • Bilateral lower lobe consolidation — dependent lung regions affected

Lungs: vomiting with altered sensorium, dependent bilateral lower-lobe change and centrilobular nodules fit aspiration pneumonitis, which is a chemical injury and does not by itself need antibiotics. Antibiotics are added if infection is likely, and routine anaerobic cover is not advised unless abscess or empyema.

Bronchoscopy and BAL

Bronchoscopy was performed with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). BAL showed no growth, which was reassuring but does not exclude aspiration pneumonitis — the initial injury is chemical, not infectious.

Indications for bronchoscopy in this case

  • Therapeutic: clearance of secretions, mucus plugging
  • Diagnostic: BAL for microbiological analysis to rule out bacterial superinfection
  • Management of atelectasis not responding to physiotherapy or recruitment manoeuvres

Technique considerations

  • FiO₂ increased to 100% prior to procedure
  • Low-pressure suction (<100 mmHg)
  • At least 100 mL saline instilled for BAL
  • Post-procedure recruitment manoeuvre may reduce atelectasis incidence

BAL results: No growth — supports aspiration pneumonitis rather than bacterial pneumonia at that time.

Tracheostomy and Weaning

The patient failed CPAP/PS trials consecutively. This is not uncommon in severe TBI with aspiration pneumonitis: the combination of impaired consciousness, weak cough, and poor lung compliance makes extubation high-risk.

Indications for tracheostomy in this patient

  • Prolonged mechanical ventilation — expected duration > 7–14 days
  • Failed weaning — consecutive CPAP/PS failures
  • Need for airway protection — GCS not improving rapidly
  • Facilitation of pulmonary toilet — retained secretions, atelectasis
  • Reduced sedation needs — easier neurological assessment

Tracheostomy was performed on Day 9–10 of admission. The timing aligns with current evidence supporting early tracheostomy (within 7–14 days) in patients with severe TBI and predicted prolonged ventilation, which may reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia, sedation requirements, and ICU length of stay.

Weaning trajectory: Following tracheostomy, the patient was gradually weaned. He later tolerated CPAP/PS weaning trials — a significant milestone that reflected improving lung compliance, adequate neurological recovery, and successful clearance of secretions. The combination of tracheostomy, reduced sedation, and daily spontaneous breathing trials formed the backbone of the weaning strategy.

Tracheostomy is not a failure of weaning. It is a strategic decision to optimise the trajectory — protecting the lung, freeing the brain from sedation, and giving the patient the best chance of meaningful recovery. — Case 007, Day 10 ICU rounds

Complications to Watch For

Tension Pneumocephalus

Neurological decline after positive pressure, CSF drainage or transport. Mount Fuji sign on CT. Urgent neurosurgical decompression.

Meningitis

Risk persists while a dural breach is open. Pneumococcus is the classic organism. Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended (Cochrane 2015).

Persistent CSF Leak

Clear nasal or ear fluid, halo sign. Most settle in 7–10 days with head elevation. Needs surgical repair if it does not settle.

Haematoma Growth

Contusion and EDH can enlarge. Repeat CT for any neurological change. Serial imaging is the safety net.

Vascular Injury

Clival and carotid-canal fractures can injure the carotid or vertebrobasilar vessels. Consider CT angiography.

Orbital Injury

Check pupils, proptosis and vision; urgent ophthalmology if abnormal.

Guidelines, Protocols & Evidence

Key sources

  • Brain Trauma Foundation, 4th edition (2017): ICP threshold 22 mmHg; CPP 60–70 mmHg; avoid hypotension and hypoxia; no steroids; no prophylactic hyperventilation.
  • Pneumocephalus reviews (Schirmer 2010; Pillai 2017): most cases resolve spontaneously; tension pneumocephalus is a neurosurgical emergency; avoid nitrous oxide, positive-pressure and nasal devices where a breach exists.
  • Dexter and Reasoner (1996): theoretical assessment of high-FiO₂ therapy for pneumocephalus.
  • Surgical management of EDH (Bullock 2006): criteria for non-operative care.
  • Cochrane (2015): prophylactic antibiotics do not reduce meningitis in basilar skull fracture.
  • ARDSNet / ESICM: Lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes (4–8 mL/kg PBW) and plateau pressure ≤ 30 cmH₂O.
  • Surviving Sepsis Campaign: Aspiration pneumonitis is not treated with antibiotics unless bacterial superinfection is suspected.

What I Keep Coming Back To

A GCS of 13 did not describe this injury; the CT did. Air in the ventricles told us a dural breach was open, and each later decision — from the route of the tube and the choice of gas to the weaning plan and the way he was transported — had to respect that. The ICP monitor told us the pressure was controlled. The serial CTs told us the contusions were resolving. The bronchoscopy told us the airway was clear. The tracheostomy told us the patient was ready for the long road of weaning. And through it all, the discipline of daily reassessment — of imaging, of ventilation, of sodium, of sedation — is what kept the trajectory moving in the right direction. Conservative management is not the absence of intervention. It is the deliberate, evidence-based decision to let the body heal while we support it, monitor it, and stay ready to act if it doesn't.

The air is not the injury, but it decides how gently everything else must be done. — Reflection
