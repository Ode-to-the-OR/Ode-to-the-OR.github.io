Guideline Update

Postpartum haemorrhage 2025: the anaesthesiologist's guide.

The first joint WHO, FIGO and ICM guideline devoted entirely to PPH — 51 recommendations, a new definition, blood loss that is measured rather than guessed, and a treatment bundle that starts everything at once. Read here for the anaesthesiologist: uterotonic haemodynamics, fibrinogen and coagulopathy, technique, and when to call the massive transfusion protocol.

Obstetric PPH Haemorrhage WHO / FIGO / ICM · Consolidated PPH guideline, 5 Oct 2025 · with ESAIC, the international uterotonic consensus and key trials

Postpartum haemorrhage remains the leading cause of maternal death worldwide, and most of those deaths are preventable. The problem is rarely the lack of a drug. It is late recognition, a stepwise ladder climbed too slowly, and teams that do not share the same plan. The 2025 consolidated guideline pulls prevention, diagnosis and treatment into one document and pushes on all three: define PPH earlier, measure blood loss objectively, and give the first-response treatments together.

In short

PPH is now measured blood loss of 500 mL or more, or 300 mL or more with abnormal vital signs — whichever comes first. Measure with a calibrated drape. First-response treatment is a bundle given at once: uterine massage, an oxytocic, tranexamic acid, IV fluids, examination of the genital tract and escalation. TXA is for treatment, not routine prevention. At caesarean, avoid a rapid oxytocin bolus. For the anaesthesiologist, fibrinogen is the early coagulation casualty — replace it when it is low, not pre-emptively — and a safe, simple technique matters as much as the drugs.

Define it and measure it

New 2025

Clinically significant PPH: measured blood loss of at least 500 mL, or at least 300 mL with signs of haemodynamic compromise — pulse above 100/min, systolic BP below 100 mmHg, diastolic BP below 60 mmHg, or shock index above 1. Start first-response treatment at whichever threshold is reached first.

The shift is from eyeballing to measuring. Use a calibrated drape for blood collection after vaginal birth, and assess uterine tone early to catch atony before the bleeding is heavy. Visual estimates under-read, and pregnancy hides shock: blood volume rises by roughly 40–50%, so a woman can lose a litre or more before the blood pressure moves. Treating at 300 mL plus a vital-sign trigger is meant to close that gap.

Tone (atony) is the commonest cause, followed by trauma, retained tissue and thrombin (coagulopathy) — the four Ts. Look for all of them from the start, because the bundle treats atony but a laceration or retained placenta needs hands and light.

Prevention: one uterotonic, given to everyone

New 2025

A quality-assured uterotonic is recommended for every birth, vaginal or caesarean. Use only one of oxytocin, carbetocin or misoprostol for prevention.

Oxytocin10 IU IM or IVFirst choice where it is stored properly. Heat degrades it, so cold-chain failure means an ineffective drug.
Carbetocin100 µg IM or IVAlternative to oxytocin. The heat-stable formulation is recommended where the cold chain cannot be guaranteed.

Not recommended

Routine prophylactic tranexamic acid for vaginal or caesarean birth is not recommended by the 2025 guideline. TXA belongs in treatment.

Prevention starts before labour. Antenatal anaemia lowers reserve and raises the cost of any bleed, and the 2025 FIGO good-practice recommendations on anaemia in pregnancy treat its correction as part of PPH prevention. Risk factors are worth recording but predict poorly — many severe PPHs happen in women with none — so every birth gets a uterotonic and every unit stays ready.

Oxytocin at caesarean: the anaesthesiologist's drug

The WHO group found too little trial evidence to prefer one oxytocin regimen at caesarean over another. It does advise against a rapid IV bolus and suggests splitting the 10 IU between a smaller bolus and an infusion. That matches the international consensus statement on uterotonics at caesarean (Anaesthesia, 2019), which recommends a low initial dose followed by a titrated infusion:

SettingInitial bolusInfusion
Elective caesarean1 IU slowly (over at least 15 s)2.5–7.5 IU/h
Caesarean in labour3 IU slowly7.5–15 IU/h
Consensus / practice · doses are starting points, and are raised for atony risk factors

A 2024 dose-finding study found that after a 1 IU bolus at elective caesarean under spinal anaesthesia, a maintenance infusion of 4.5 IU/h was enough in 90% of women. Oxytocin causes vasodilation, hypotension and tachycardia, and a 5 IU bolus is the classic cause of both. In women with cardiac disease, use the slowest, smallest-bolus approach, and give a second-line agent early if tone is poor rather than escalating oxytocin without limit.

Treatment: the bundle, all at once

New 2025

When PPH is diagnosed, give all of the first-response elements together, not in sequence: Massage the uterus, give an Oxytocic, give Tranexamic acid, start IV fluids, and Examine the genital tract and Escalate care — the E-MOTIVE bundle. Ideally every element starts within about 15 minutes of diagnosis.

The evidence is the E-MOTIVE cluster-randomised trial (NEJM, 2023; about 200,000 women in four African countries). Early detection with a drape plus the bundle cut the composite of severe PPH (1000 mL or more), laparotomy or death from 4.3% to 1.6%. WHO adopted the bundle in 2023, and the 2025 guideline consolidates it.

  • Uterine massage while the other steps are organised. It is free, immediate and helps while drugs are drawn up.
  • Oxytocic. Oxytocin IV is first-line for treatment. If bleeding persists, move to an alternate uterotonic early (table below) instead of waiting.
  • Tranexamic acid: 1 g IV over about 10 minutes as soon as PPH is diagnosed and within 3 hours of birth, in addition to uterotonics. A second 1 g dose is given if bleeding continues after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours (WOMAN trial). Do not wait for laboratory results. Keep the cumulative dose within the usual limit, because high doses are linked to seizures and renal effects.
  • IV fluids: isotonic crystalloid to start, with blood early if loss continues. Excess crystalloid dilutes clotting factors, and obstetric evidence for permissive hypotension is lacking, so aim to restore perfusion rather than a specific low pressure.
  • Examine and escalate: inspect the cervix, vagina and perineum, remove retained placenta or tissue, and call the senior obstetrician, senior anaesthetist, blood bank and theatre early.

Magnesium sulphate, given for pre-eclampsia, relaxes the uterus and makes atony more likely. A uterine relaxant volatile at high concentration does the same, which is why many anaesthetists keep the volatile at or below 0.5 MAC or switch to TIVA once the baby is delivered.

Declare a major obstetric haemorrhage — and call the massive transfusion protocol — when loss is continuing and large (a litre or more with ongoing bleeding), when the patient is haemodynamically unstable, or when you are concerned about coagulopathy. Waiting for a number is how time is lost. See the massive transfusion protocol page for products, calcium, temperature and targets, which apply in obstetrics too.

The anaesthesiologist's role

This section is consensus and practice, not WHO text. It is where the anaesthetic decisions that change outcome sit.

Be ready before delivery

  • Know the risk flags: previous PPH, placenta praevia or accreta, multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios, antenatal anaemia, prolonged or augmented labour, pre-eclampsia, and anticoagulation. Risk prediction is imperfect, so be prepared for the unexpected too.
  • Access and blood: two large-bore IV lines in higher-risk women, a current group and screen, and cross-matched blood for the highest-risk cases. Know where emergency-release blood is and how fast it arrives.
  • Drugs and equipment: uterotonics drawn up, a vasopressor ready, a blood warmer, and cell salvage set up when risk is high (for example, placenta accreta spectrum).
  • Position: left uterine displacement until the baby is delivered.

Choosing the technique

  • Neuraxial is fine for caesarean when the patient is stable and coagulation is normal. Avoid it, or avoid topping up an existing epidural, in a woman who is hypovolaemic, haemodynamically unstable or coagulopathic.
  • General anaesthesia is the safer choice when the patient is unstable, bleeding briskly or coagulopathic, or when the block is failing. Rapid sequence induction with a reduced-dose agent (ketamine or etomidate are common choices) and a vasopressor running; expect post-induction hypotension. Pre-oxygenate well — oxygen reserve is lower in pregnancy.
  • Maintenance: keep the volatile at or below about 0.5 MAC, or use TIVA, so as not to relax the uterus, and ensure adequate depth with processed EEG if awareness is a concern.

Resuscitation

  • Give blood early rather than large volumes of crystalloid, through a warmer, and use a rapid infuser if loss is brisk. Insert an arterial line when the patient is unstable and it does not delay care.
  • Vasopressors (phenylephrine or noradrenaline) bridge while volume arrives. They do not replace it.
  • Cell salvage is an accepted option in obstetric haemorrhage with a leucocyte-depletion filter. Remember anti-D prophylaxis for RhD-negative women whose baby is RhD-positive.
  • Keep her warm and keep her calcium normal. Both matter more than they sound (see the MTP page).

Coagulopathy in PPH: fibrinogen first

Consensus / practice · ESAIC

Measure fibrinogen in the bleeding parturient — a level below 2 g/L identifies women at risk of progressing to severe PPH. Do not replace fibrinogen pre-emptively. In ongoing PPH with hypofibrinogenaemia, replace it. If fibrinogen is above 2 g/L or FIBTEM A5 above 12 mm, replacement is not indicated. In severe PPH, use a viscoelastic-guided protocol if you have one. ESAIC severe perioperative bleeding guideline, obstetric section.

Fibrinogen is the first clotting factor to fall to a critical level in PPH, through dilution, consumption and hyperfibrinolysis. That is why it is the first thing to check. But low fibrinogen is not universal. In a UK series of 203 women with major obstetric haemorrhage, only 23% had a FIBTEM A5 below 12 mm — an argument against formulaic "shock packs" and for measuring. A randomised trial that gave fibrinogen concentrate early (OBS2, using a FIBTEM A5 of 15 mm or below as the trigger) showed no reduction in blood-product use, which is why the advice is to replace a proven deficiency, not to give fibrinogen on spec.

Coagulopathy can come before the haemorrhage in abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, sepsis and HELLP syndrome, so a woman who bleeds out of proportion to her visible loss in these conditions needs early laboratory and viscoelastic testing.

Special situations

  • Pre-eclampsia: avoid ergometrine; magnesium raises the atony risk; thrombocytopenia or HELLP limits neuraxial blocks and raises bleeding risk; be careful with fluid volumes, but do not under-resuscitate a woman who is bleeding.
  • Cardiac disease: small slow oxytocin boluses or carbetocin, early invasive monitoring, and avoid sudden vasodilation and fluid overload together.
  • Placenta accreta spectrum: plan delivery in a centre with blood bank, interventional radiology, ICU and an experienced multidisciplinary team. Arrange cross-matched blood, large-bore access, an arterial line, and cell salvage in advance.
  • Anaemic patient: physiological reserve is lower; transfuse earlier on physiology and treat the anaemia afterwards with iron.
  • Declined transfusion: agree a plan antenatally, optimise haemoglobin, plan for cell salvage and early definitive surgery.

After the bleed

Monitor in a high-dependency or intensive care setting if bleeding was major. Restart thromboprophylaxis as soon as bleeding is controlled — post-PPH women are at high thrombotic risk. Treat anaemia (IV iron is often needed), tell her what happened, and debrief the team. Record measured blood loss, the time of each step, and the products given. These are the numbers you will audit.

PPH at a glance

Recognize (t = 0)

Measured blood loss at least 500 mL, or at least 300 mL with pulse above 100, SBP below 100, DBP below 60 or shock index above 1. Check tone, then look for trauma, tissue and thrombin causes.

Call

Senior obstetrician, senior anaesthetist, midwife in charge, blood bank and theatre. Say "postpartum haemorrhage" out loud.

Bundle — start all together
  • Massage the uterus
  • Oxytocin (or the next uterotonic if oxytocin already given)
  • Tranexamic acid 1 g IV over 10 min, within 3 h of birth
  • Isotonic crystalloid, two large-bore lines, blood early
  • Examine the genital tract; remove tissue; escalate
Anaesthetist's tasks

Left uterine displacement, blood warmer, calcium, labs and fibrinogen (or FIBTEM), technique chosen for stability (GA if unstable), volatile at or below 0.5 MAC, vasopressor bridge, arterial line.

Escalate

Not responding: second-line uterotonics, balloon tamponade, theatre for sutures or ligation or hysterectomy. Ongoing or unstable: call the massive transfusion protocol.

Afterwards

HDU or ICU if major, thromboprophylaxis when bleeding stops, treat anaemia, debrief and audit.

The lesson that doesn't show up in any table

The 2025 guideline's real message is about time, not drugs. Measuring blood loss gets the diagnosis made earlier, and the bundle gets treatment started in minutes. Most of the drugs are old; what changed is that they are given together, before the woman is in shock. The anaesthesiologist's contribution is to keep her physiology safe while that happens — choose the technique for stability, protect the circulation from the drugs, and replace fibrinogen and blood in time.
