Module 01 of 10
Physiology of Acid–Base Balance
The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, the bicarbonate buffer system, and the three defence systems against pH disturbance.
The Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation pH = 6.1 + log ([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PaCO₂))

At normal values: HCO₃⁻ 24 mmol/L, PaCO₂ 40 mmHg → pH = 6.1 + log (24 / 1.2) = 6.1 + log 20 = 7.40.

The Bicarbonate Buffer System

The bicarbonate system is the most important extracellular buffer. It consists of carbon dioxide (CO₂) and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), linked by the reaction:

Carbonic Anhydrase–Catalysed Reaction CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
Carbonic anhydrase accelerates this reaction in red blood cells; the reaction is slow in plasma
  • HCO₃⁻ is the most abundant buffer base in blood — normal 22–26 mmol/L
  • PaCO₂ is controlled by ventilation — the lungs are the rapid responder
  • HCO₃⁻ is controlled by the kidneys — the slow responder (hours to days)
  • The ratio of HCO₃⁻ to dissolved CO₂ (0.03 × PaCO₂) must remain 20:1 to maintain pH 7.40

Module 02 of 10
Pre-Analytical Errors
The sample is only as good as the technique. Air bubbles, delayed analysis, and heparin dilution are the most common sources of error.

The Pre-Analytical Phase — The Leading Source of Error

Studies show that 25% of requisition forms are incomplete, and pre-analytical errors are the primary source of inaccurate blood gas results. The most important variables are:

ErrorEffect on ResultsPrevention
Air bubblesPaO₂ falsely ↑; PaCO₂ falsely ↓; pH falsely ↑Expel all bubbles immediately; cap syringe; do not shake
Delayed analysisPaCO₂ ↑; pH ↓; PaO₂ ↓ (at room temp); PaO₂ ↑ (on ice)Analyse within 15 minutes (ice) or 30 minutes (room temp, plastic syringe)
Excess heparinDilutional effect: PaCO₂ ↓; HCO₃⁻ ↓; pH alteredUse pre-heparinised syringes; heparin <10% of total volume; mix gently
HaemolysisK⁺ falsely ↑↑; PaCO₂ ↓; pH ↑Avoid vigorous shaking; do not use negative pressure; use appropriate needle size
Venous admixturePaO₂ ↓↓; PaCO₂ ↑; pH ↓Confirm arterial puncture; avoid tourniquet for arterial samples; check for pulsatile flow
Improper mixingClotting; inconsistent resultsRoll syringe between palms gently; do not shake
⚠ Air Bubbles — The Most Critical Error Even a micro-bubble can falsely elevate PaO₂ and falsely lower PaCO₂. The sample must be sealed immediately and all air expelled. This is the single most important pre-analytical step.

Transport and Storage

  • Ideal: Analyse within 15 minutes on ice (slows metabolism)
  • Acceptable: Plastic syringe at room temperature — clinically acceptable for up to 30 minutes
  • Delayed: PaCO₂ rises progressively, pH falls; temperature amplifies these changes
  • On ice: PaO₂ may increase (due to reduced metabolism) — note this when interpreting

Patient-Related Pre-Analytical Factors

  • Stability: Patient should be at rest for 5–10 minutes; agitation or crying causes hyperventilation → falsely low PaCO₂, falsely high pH
  • Oxygen therapy: Document FiO₂, flow rate, and ventilator settings; do not change oxygen immediately before sampling — wait 15–30 minutes for steady state
  • Temperature: Record patient temperature — results should be corrected to 37°C for accurate interpretation
  • Haemoglobin: Record Hb — required for calculating oxygen content and delivery

Module 03 of 10
Stepwise Interpretation
A systematic approach — from pH to compensation to anion gap. Never interpret an ABG without a structured method.

The Six-Step Method

Step 1 — Assess pH

  • Acidaemia: pH <7.35
  • Alkalaemia: pH >7.45
  • Normal pH: May still have a mixed disorder (e.g., metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis)

Step 2 — Identify the Primary Disorder

pHPaCO₂HCO₃⁻Primary Disorder
↓↑Normal/↑Respiratory Acidosis
↑↓Normal/↓Respiratory Alkalosis
↓Normal/↓↓Metabolic Acidosis
↑Normal/↑↑Metabolic Alkalosis
The Primary Driver Look at which parameter is most abnormal relative to its normal range. If PaCO₂ is the primary abnormality, it is a respiratory disorder. If HCO₃⁻ is the primary abnormality, it is a metabolic disorder.

Step 6 — Assess Oxygenation

  • PaO₂: Normal 80–100 mmHg on room air
  • P/F ratio: PaO₂ / FiO₂ — normal >400; <300 suggests significant shunt/V-Q mismatch
  • A–a gradient: Normal 5–20 mmHg; elevated indicates gas exchange defect
  • Expected PaO₂: ~5 × FiO₂ (rough estimate)
  • SpO₂: Normal 95–100%; correlates with PaO₂ via the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve

Compensation Formulas
The expected responses — and when they are inappropriate, indicating a mixed disorder.

Expected Compensation — The Formulas

DisorderExpected CompensationFormulaInterpretation
Metabolic AcidosisRespiratory (↓PaCO₂)PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2Winter's formula. If measured PaCO₂ is higher → concurrent respiratory acidosis; if lower → concurrent respiratory alkalosis
Metabolic AlkalosisRespiratory (↑PaCO₂)PaCO₂ = (0.7 × HCO₃⁻) + 20 (range ±5)If measured PaCO₂ is outside range → mixed disorder
Acute Respiratory AcidosisMetabolic (↑HCO₃⁻)HCO₃⁻ ↑ 1 mmol/L for every 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂Compensation is small and immediate
Chronic Respiratory AcidosisMetabolic (↑HCO₃⁻)HCO₃⁻ ↑ 3.5 mmol/L for every 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂Renal compensation; takes 3–5 days (some sources use 4 mmol/L)
Acute Respiratory AlkalosisMetabolic (↓HCO₃⁻)HCO₃⁻ ↓ 2 mmol/L for every 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂Compensation is small
Chronic Respiratory AlkalosisMetabolic (↓HCO₃⁻)HCO₃⁻ ↓ 5 mmol/L for every 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂Renal compensation
Winter's Formula — The Most Important Expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2

Example: HCO₃⁻ 16 → expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × 16) + 8 = 32 ± 2 (range 30–34). If measured PaCO₂ is 45 → concurrent respiratory acidosis. If measured PaCO₂ is 22 → concurrent respiratory alkalosis.

Acute vs Chronic — Respiratory Disorders

DisorderExpected HCO₃⁻ Change (Acute)Expected HCO₃⁻ Change (Chronic)
Respiratory Acidosis↑ 1 for every 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂↑ 3.5 for every 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂
Respiratory Alkalosis↓ 2 for every 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂↓ 5 for every 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂

Example: PaCO₂ 60 (↑20 from normal). Acute expected HCO₃⁻ = 24 + 2 = 26. Chronic expected HCO₃⁻ = 24 + 7 = 31. If measured HCO₃⁻ is 32 → chronic respiratory acidosis.

When Compensation is Inappropriate

  • Measured value outside expected range: A second disorder is present
  • Example: Metabolic acidosis with PaCO₂ higher than Winter's formula predicts → concurrent respiratory acidosis (e.g., respiratory muscle fatigue)
  • Example: Respiratory acidosis with HCO₃⁻ higher than expected for chronicity → concurrent metabolic alkalosis (e.g., diuretic use)
  • Normal pH with abnormal PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻: Mixed disorder — the two abnormalities are opposing
⚠ Compensation is Never Complete for Metabolic Alkalosis Respiratory compensation for metabolic alkalosis is limited by hypoventilation — the body will not tolerate significant hypoxaemia. A normal pH with elevated HCO₃⁻ and elevated PaCO₂ suggests a mixed disorder, not full compensation.
Module 05 of 10
The Anion Gap
The cornerstone of metabolic acidosis evaluation — and the albumin correction that is too often forgotten.

Calculating the Anion Gap

Anion Gap AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)
Normal: 8–12 mmol/L (±2) in most laboratories; some cite up to 16 — use your local reference range

The Albumin Correction

Albumin is the major unmeasured anion. In hypoalbuminaemia, the anion gap is falsely low. Correct the normal range:

Albumin-Corrected Anion Gap Corrected AG = AG + 0.25 × (44 − Albumin) [albumin in g/L]
Alternative convention: for every 10 g/L decrease in albumin, reduce the normal AG by 3. Coefficients vary — use your local formula
  • Example: Na 140, Cl 105, HCO₃ 18 → AG = 17. Albumin 20 g/L (down 24 from 44). Corrected normal AG = 12 − (24 × 0.3) ≈ 4.8. So the measured AG of 17 is even more elevated relative to the corrected normal
  • Always correct the AG for albumin — a "normal" AG in a hypoalbuminaemic patient may be a hidden high AG acidosis

High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis — Causes

Normal Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis — Causes

  • GI losses of HCO₃⁻: Diarrhoea, ileostomy, fistula, acetazolamide
  • Renal tubular acidosis: Types 1, 2, and 4
  • Hyperchloraemia: Large-volume normal saline resuscitation
  • Recovery from DKA: Ketones excreted, Cl⁻ retained
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors: Acetazolamide, topiramate
Module 06 of 10
The Delta Ratio
When the anion gap is elevated — does the change in AG explain the change in HCO₃⁻? Or is there a second disorder?

Calculating the Delta Ratio

Delta Ratio Delta Ratio = ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻ = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO₃⁻)
Normal AG = 12; normal HCO₃⁻ = 24

Interpretation

Delta RatioInterpretationClinical Significance
<0.8Combined High AG + Normal AG Metabolic AcidosisBoth HAGMA and NAGMA present — e.g., DKA + diarrhoea
1–2Uncomplicated High AG Metabolic AcidosisPure HAGMA — the AG rise explains the HCO₃⁻ fall
>2High AG Metabolic Acidosis + Metabolic AlkalosisHCO₃⁻ is higher than expected — concurrent metabolic alkalosis (e.g., vomiting, diuretics)
✦ Worked Example Na 140, Cl 100, HCO₃⁻ 16 → AG = 24. ΔAG = 24 − 12 = 12. ΔHCO₃⁻ = 24 − 16 = 8. Delta ratio = 12/8 = 1.5. Interpretation: uncomplicated high AG metabolic acidosis (e.g., lactic acidosis).

Osmolar Gap — When to Calculate

If a high AG metabolic acidosis is present with an unexplained cause, calculate the osmolar gap:

Osmolar Gap Osmolar Gap = Measured Osmolality − Calculated Osmolality

Calculated Osmolality = 2[Na⁺] + Urea + Glucose (all in mmol/L)
Normal <10 mOsm/kg. Elevated gap suggests toxic alcohol (methanol, ethylene glycol)
Module 07 of 10
Electrolytes on Blood Gas Analysis
Sodium and potassium from the blood gas analyser — and why they may disagree with the laboratory.

Sodium and Potassium — Point-of-Care vs Laboratory

Blood gas analysers use direct potentiometry (DP) on whole blood, while central laboratories use indirect potentiometry (IP) on diluted plasma/serum. This fundamental difference explains most discrepancies.

MethodSampleDilutionInterference
Direct Potentiometry (POC)Whole bloodNoneNot affected by protein/lipid; affected by haemolysis (K⁺)
Indirect Potentiometry (Lab)Plasma/serumYesFalsely low Na⁺ in hyperproteinaemia/hyperlipidaemia (pseudohyponatraemia)
⚠ Pseudohyperkalaemia from Haemolysis The most common POC potassium error is haemolysis — red cells release K⁺, falsely elevating the result. Unlike the laboratory, POC analysers cannot inspect the sample for haemolysis. If K⁺ is unexpectedly high, repeat with a fresh, carefully collected sample.

Correcting Sodium for Hyperglycaemia

In hyperglycaemia, sodium is diluted by osmotic water shift. Corrected sodium:

Corrected Na⁺ Corrected Na⁺ = Measured Na⁺ + 0.3 × (Glucose − 5.5) [glucose in mmol/L]
Or: add 1.6 mmol/L to Na⁺ for every 5.5 mmol/L glucose above normal

Potassium — Artefactual Elevation

  • Haemolysis: Most common cause — repeat sample
  • Prolonged tourniquet: Venous stasis → K⁺ rises — not relevant for arterial samples
  • Delayed processing: Cellular K⁺ leaks — analyse promptly
  • Thrombocytosis/leucocytosis: Cells release K⁺ during clotting — use heparinised sample
  • True hyperkalaemia: Renal failure, acidosis (K⁺ shifts out of cells), tissue breakdown

Chloride and the Strong Ion Gap

  • Chloride: Normal 96–106 mmol/L
  • Hyperchloraemic acidosis: Large-volume 0.9% saline → normal AG metabolic acidosis
  • Strong Ion Difference (SID): Na⁺ + K⁺ − Cl⁻ — an alternative approach to acid–base; normal ~40
  • Strong Ion Gap (SIG): Unmeasured anions — elevated in lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, uraemia

Lactate — Type A vs Type B

TypeMechanismCauses
Type ATissue hypoxia — anaerobic metabolismShock, cardiac arrest, severe anaemia, CO poisoning
Type BNormal oxygen delivery — aerobic glycolysis or impaired clearanceSepsis, liver failure, thiamine deficiency, metformin, malignancy, post-cardiac surgery
Lactate Clearance Lactate clearance ≥10–20% over 2–6 hours indicates adequate resuscitation. Persistent hyperlactataemia is associated with worse outcomes.
Module 08 of 10
Special Situations
Temperature correction, venous blood gases, mixed disorders, and the traps that catch the unwary.

Temperature Correction

  • ABG analysers report values at 37°C by default
  • In hypothermia, PaCO₂ and PaO₂ are falsely low (gas is more soluble at lower temperature); pH is falsely high
  • In hyperthermia, the opposite occurs
  • Clinical relevance: In therapeutic hypothermia or severe accidental hypothermia, uncorrected values may mislead. However, most guidelines recommend interpreting at 37°C for consistency, and correcting only when the discrepancy would change management

Venous Blood Gas — What It Can and Cannot Tell You

ParameterCan Be Estimated?Formula / Rule
pHYesArterial ≈ venous + 0.02–0.04
PaCO₂YesArterial ≈ venous − 3–8 mmHg
HCO₃⁻YesReliable within 1–2 mmol/L
PaO₂NoNo reliable correlation
Modern Conversion Formulas A 2026 study of 5,419 paired samples derived formulas to transform VBG to ABG. For pCO₂: linear regression (r = 0.93) with 95% limits of agreement −5.6 to +5.5 mmHg. For pH: r = 0.94, limits −0.05 to +0.04. These formulas provide reasonable estimates but do not replace ABG when accurate PaO₂ is required.

Mixed Disorders — Recognising the Patterns

PatternExampleClue
Metabolic acidosis + respiratory acidosisCardiac arrest; severe COPD with lactic acidosisPaCO₂ higher than Winter's formula predicts
Metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosisSepsis; salicylate toxicityPaCO₂ lower than Winter's formula predicts

The "Normal pH" Trap

  • A normal pH does not exclude a serious acid–base disorder
  • Mixed disorders can cancel out — e.g., metabolic acidosis (pH ↓) + respiratory alkalosis (pH ↑) = normal pH
  • Always check the anion gap, delta ratio, and compensation formulas even when pH is normal
  • If pH is normal but PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are both abnormal → mixed disorder

Clinical Scenarios — Putting It Together

ABG: pH 7.15, PaCO₂ 22, HCO₃⁻ 8, Na 132, Cl 98, Glucose 28 mmol/L, Ketones +++.

Step 1: Acidaemia.
Step 2: HCO₃⁻ 8 (low) → metabolic acidosis. PaCO₂ 22 (low) → respiratory compensation.
Step 3: Winter's: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5×8 + 8 = 20 ± 2. Measured 22 → appropriate compensation.
Step 4: AG = 132 − (98+8) = 26. High AG metabolic acidosis.
Step 5: Delta ratio = (26−12)/(24−8) = 14/16 = 0.875. Uncomplicated HAGMA (close to 1).
Diagnosis: Diabetic ketoacidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation.
ABG: pH 7.48, PaCO₂ 20, HCO₃⁻ 15, Na 140, Cl 105.

Step 1: Alkalaemia.
Step 2: HCO₃⁻ 15 (low) → metabolic acidosis. PaCO₂ 20 (low) → respiratory alkalosis. Both primary.
Step 3: Winter's: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5×15 + 8 = 30.5 ± 2. Measured 20 → much lower → concurrent respiratory alkalosis.
Step 4: AG = 140 − (105+15) = 20. High AG metabolic acidosis.
Step 5: Delta ratio = (20−12)/(24−15) = 8/9 = 0.89. HAGMA.
Diagnosis: Mixed high AG metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis. Classic for salicylate toxicity.
ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 70, HCO₃⁻ 32, PaO₂ 55.

Step 1: Acidaemia.
Step 2: PaCO₂ 70 (high) → respiratory acidosis. HCO₃⁻ 32 (high) → metabolic compensation.
Step 3: Expected HCO₃⁻ for chronic respiratory acidosis: 24 + (30/10)×3.5 = 34.5. Measured 32 → less than chronic expectation, more than acute (24+3=27). Acute-on-chronic.
Step 4: AG normal.
Diagnosis: Acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis. The HCO₃⁻ is elevated but not fully compensated for the degree of hypercapnia.

Memory, Pitfalls & Synthesis
The revision tool — a single-page algorithm, the formula sheet, the top traps, and a synthesis case that uses every step.

1 · The One-Page Algorithm

  • 1. pH — Acidaemia (<7.35) or alkalaemia (>7.45)?
  • 2. Primary — Is PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ the primary driver?
  • 3. Compensation — Winter's formula for metabolic acidosis; expected HCO₃⁻ for respiratory disorders (acute vs chronic).
  • 4. Anion gap — If metabolic acidosis: AG = Na − (Cl + HCO₃); correct for albumin.
  • 5. Delta ratio — If elevated AG: ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻; 1–2 = uncomplicated HAGMA; <0.8 = mixed HAGMA + NAGMA; >2 = HAGMA + metabolic alkalosis.
  • 6. Oxygenation — PaO₂, P/F ratio, A–a gradient, FiO₂.

3 · The Top 10 Traps

A normal pH with abnormal PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ in opposite directions = mixed disorder. Always calculate the anion gap and delta ratio even when pH is normal.
A "normal" AG in a hypoalbuminaemic patient may be a hidden high AG acidosis. Always correct the AG for albumin (or lower the expected normal range).
If the AG rise does not explain the HCO₃⁻ fall (delta ratio <0.8), there is a concurrent normal AG metabolic acidosis (e.g., DKA + diarrhoea).
If the HCO₃⁻ is higher than expected for the degree of AG elevation (delta ratio >2), there is a concurrent metabolic alkalosis (e.g., vomiting, diuretics).
The most common POC potassium error. If K⁺ is unexpectedly high, repeat with a fresh, carefully collected sample. POC analysers cannot detect haemolysis.
Even a micro-bubble can distort the result. Expel all air immediately, cap the syringe, and analyse promptly.
A venous sample shows low PaO₂, high PaCO₂, and low pH. Confirm arterial puncture; check for pulsatile flow; avoid tourniquet for arterial samples.
Acute respiratory acidosis raises HCO₃⁻ by 1 per 10 mmHg; chronic raises it by 3.5 per 10 mmHg. Misclassifying acute as chronic (or vice versa) changes the interpretation.
In hyperproteinaemia or hyperlipidaemia, laboratory (indirect) sodium is falsely low. POC (direct) sodium is not affected. If the lab and POC disagree, consider this.
Type B lactate occurs with normal oxygen delivery — sepsis, liver failure, thiamine deficiency, metformin, malignancy. Do not assume all lactate elevation is hypoperfusion.

4 · The Synthesis Case

A patient with vomiting and sepsis

ABG: pH 7.36, PaCO₂ 30, HCO₃⁻ 16, Na 138, Cl 98, Albumin 22 g/L, Lactate 6.2.

Step 1: pH 7.36 — within normal range, but at the lower end.
Step 2: HCO₃⁻ 16 (low) → metabolic acidosis. PaCO₂ 30 (low) → respiratory compensation. But pH is nearly normal — this suggests a mixed disorder.
Step 3: Winter's: expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × 16) + 8 = 32 ± 2. Measured 30 → slightly lower → concurrent respiratory alkalosis (or appropriate compensation at the lower end).
Step 4: AG = 138 − (98 + 16) = 24. High AG metabolic acidosis.
Step 5: Albumin 22 → corrected normal AG ≈ 12 − (22 × 0.3) ≈ 5.4. Measured AG 24 is markedly elevated even after correction.
Step 6: Delta ratio = (24 − 12) / (24 − 16) = 12 / 8 = 1.5. Uncomplicated HAGMA.
Step 7: But the pH is nearly normal — how? The metabolic acidosis (from lactate) is offset by a metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting). The delta ratio of 1.5 suggests the AG rise is greater than the HCO₃⁻ fall — consistent with a concurrent metabolic alkalosis.

Diagnosis: Mixed high AG metabolic acidosis (lactic acidosis from sepsis) + metabolic alkalosis (vomiting) + respiratory compensation. The near-normal pH is deceptive — two opposing disorders are cancelling out.
✦ The Final Rule Never interpret a single number in isolation. The pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, anion gap, albumin, delta ratio, and clinical context must all be read together. A normal pH can hide a serious mixed disorder — and a normal anion gap can hide a high AG acidosis in a hypoalbuminaemic patient.
