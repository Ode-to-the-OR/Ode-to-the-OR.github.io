⚠ Serum Values Do Not Equal Total Body Stores Serum potassium reflects only ~2% of total body potassium. Serum magnesium is a poor marker of total body magnesium — a normal level does not exclude deficiency. Serum calcium must be interpreted with albumin (or measured as ionised calcium). Sodium measured by indirect potentiometry (laboratory chemistry) is falsely low in hyperproteinaemia/hyperlipidaemia; direct potentiometry (blood gas) is not affected [citation:6][citation:13].

The Rate of Correction — The Central Principle

The most important principle in electrolyte correction is that the rate matters as much as the target. Correcting too slowly prolongs the disturbance; correcting too rapidly can cause permanent neurological injury or cardiac arrest.

ElectrolyteMaximum Correction RateConsequence of Over-Correction
Sodium6–8 mmol/L per 24 h (chronic hyponatraemia; some sources allow up to 10)Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)
Potassium10–20 mmol/h (peripheral); up to 40 mmol/h (central, monitored)Cardiac arrhythmia; rebound hypokalaemia [citation:14]
CalciumDo not exceed 0.5 mmol/L/h in hypercalcaemia correctionCardiac arrhythmia; soft tissue calcification
Magnesium1–2 g/h (routine); faster only in emergenciesHypotension, respiratory depression, loss in urine
PhosphateDo not exceed 0.5 mmol/kg over 6–12 hHypocalcaemia, metastatic calcification

The Golden Rule Correct the underlying cause first, and the electrolyte second. Hyponatraemia from SIADH will not stay corrected if the SIADH persists. Hyperkalaemia from renal failure will recur unless dialysis is initiated. Hypercalcaemia from malignancy requires treating the malignancy — fluids and bisphosphonates buy time.

SIADH — A Diagnosis of Exclusion

  • Essential criteria: Serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg; urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg; clinical euvolaemia; urine Na⁺ >30 mmol/L (on normal salt intake); normal thyroid and adrenal function
  • Exclude adrenal insufficiency: Early morning cortisol <140 nmol/L is diagnostic; <420 nmol/L requires a corticotropin stimulation test; >300 nmol/L excludes glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency
  • Causes: Malignancy (especially lung), pulmonary disease, CNS disorders, drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide), idiopathic
⚠ Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS) Rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia can cause ODS (central pontine myelinolysis) — presenting days after correction with dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, and "locked-in" syndrome. Risk is highest with chronic hyponatraemia (<120 mmol/L for >48 h), alcoholism, malnutrition, hypokalaemia, and liver disease. The "rule of 6" is a practical safety framework: 6 mmol/L in 6 hours for severe symptoms, then stop; ≤6 mmol/L per day for safety [citation:10].

Hypernatraemia

Hypernatraemia (>145 mmol/L) reflects a deficit of total body water relative to sodium. It almost always involves impaired thirst or limited access to water.

TypeCausesManagement
HypovolaemicDiarrhoea, vomiting, osmotic diuresis, diuretics0.9% saline first if haemodynamically unstable; then hypotonic fluid
EuvolaemicDiabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic), pure water lossFree water replacement (oral/NG or 5% dextrose); treat DI
HypervolaemicHypertonic saline, sodium administration, mineralocorticoid excessStop sodium; diuretics; dialysis if renal failure
⚠ Correct Hypernatraemia Slowly Chronic hypernatraemia must be corrected at ≤0.5 mmol/L/h (≤10–12 mmol/L/day) to avoid cerebral oedema. Use the free water deficit formula to plan the volume; replace half in the first 24 h, the remainder over the next 24–48 h.

Hypokalaemia

  • Severity: Mild 3.0–3.4; moderate 2.5–2.9; severe <2.5 mmol/L
  • ECG: U waves, flattened T waves, ST depression, prolonged QT, arrhythmias
  • Correction: Oral KCl preferred if tolerated (40–80 mmol/day in divided doses); IV KCl for severe or symptomatic (20–40 mmol over 1–2 h via peripheral line, max 10 mmol/h; central line for up to 40 mmol/h with monitoring) [citation:14]
  • Always check magnesium: Hypomagnesaemia causes refractory hypokalaemia — replace Mg first (0.5–1 g/h) [citation:14]
  • Causes: GI loss (vomiting, diarrhoea, NG suction), renal loss (diuretics, Conn's, RTA), insulin, β-agonists, alkalosis

Mild2.6–3.010.4–12.0Often asymptomaticTreat cause; hydration; monitor
Moderate3.0–3.512.0–14.0Polyuria, constipation, confusion, fatigueIV saline 0.9% (2–4 L/day); bisphosphonate

  • First step: IV 0.9% saline — restore volume, dilute calcium, promote renal excretion (up to 3–5 L/24 h in severe cases)
  • Bisphosphonates: Zoledronic acid 4 mg IV or pamidronate 60–90 mg IV — onset 12–24 h, effect lasts weeks
  • Calcitonin: 4 IU/kg SC/IM q12h — rapid onset (hours), but tachyphylaxis within 48 h; used for severe/crisis
  • Steroids: For granulomatous disease or lymphoma-associated hypercalcaemia
  • Dialysis: For refractory hypercalcaemia with renal failure or cardiac instability

Hypocalcaemia

  • Symptoms: Perioral tingling, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek's sign, Trousseau's sign, laryngospasm, seizures, QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmia
  • Severe/crisis (<1.0 mmol/L ionised or symptomatic): Calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL IV over 10 min (provides 2.2–6.6 mmol Ca²⁺), then infusion; calcium chloride 10% 5–10 mL via central line for cardiac arrest (provides 3.4–6.8 mmol Ca²⁺)
  • Chronic: Oral calcium carbonate + vitamin D (alfacalcidol or calcitriol if renal failure)
  • Always check magnesium: Hypomagnesaemia causes refractory hypocalcaemia — replace Mg first
  • Causes: Hypoparathyroidism (post-surgical), vitamin D deficiency, CKD, pancreatitis, massive transfusion (citrate binding), respiratory alkalosis (ionised Ca falls)

⚠ Magnesium and Potassium — The Refractory Link Hypomagnesaemia causes renal potassium wasting and impaired cellular K⁺ uptake. Hypokalaemia will not correct until magnesium is replaced. The same applies to hypocalcaemia — low Mg impairs PTH secretion and action. When repleting potassium, co-administer magnesium 0.5–1 g/h [citation:14].
  • Causes: GI loss (diarrhoea, malabsorption, pancreatitis, PPIs), renal loss (diuretics, aminoglycosides, amphotericin, cisplatin, calcineurin inhibitors), alcohol, uncontrolled diabetes
  • ECG: Prolonged QT, torsades de pointes, ST depression, U waves
  • In CKD: Reduce dose by 25–50%; monitor closely; specialist input in AKI or severe CKD

Hypermagnesaemia

  • Causes: Usually renal failure or iatrogenic (Mg therapy, Mg-containing antacids/laxatives)
  • Features: Hyporeflexia, weakness, hypotension, bradycardia; severe → respiratory depression, cardiac arrest
  • Management: Stop all magnesium sources; calcium gluconate 1 g IV (antagonises cardiac/neuromuscular effects); IV fluids ± loop diuretic if renal function adequate; dialysis if severe or renal failure

Bicarbonate Therapy — When to Use

  • Not routinely recommended for metabolic acidosis with pH >7.1 — the evidence does not support improved outcomes and there are risks (hypernatraemia, hypercapnia, hypokalaemia, intracellular acidosis, overshoot alkalosis)
  • Consider for pH <7.1 with haemodynamic instability, severe hyperkalaemia, or certain toxin ingestions (salicylate, tricyclic)
  • In cardiac arrest: Not routinely recommended; may be considered for prolonged arrest or hyperkalaemia
  • In renal failure: Sodium bicarbonate is preferred over trometamol (which contains phosphate)

Metabolic Alkalosis

  • Causes: Vomiting/NG suction (loss of H⁺ and Cl⁻), diuretics, hypokalaemia, mineralocorticoid excess, alkali administration
  • Assessment: Urine Cl⁻ <20 mmol/L = chloride-responsive (volume depletion); >20 = chloride-resistant (mineralocorticoid excess, severe hypokalaemia)
  • Chloride-responsive: 0.9% saline + KCl replacement
  • Chloride-resistant: Treat cause; KCl; spironolactone; acetazolamide (if fluid overload)
  • Severe (pH >7.55): Acetazolamide 250–500 mg IV; or dilute HCl via central line in extreme cases; dialysis

  • Causes: Renal failure (most common), tumour lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, excessive phosphate administration
  • Consequences: Hypocalcaemia (precipitation), metastatic calcification, vascular calcification
  • Management: Phosphate binders (calcium acetate, sevelamer) with meals; dietary restriction; dialysis for severe or renal failure; treat tumour lysis/rhabdomyolysis
Module 08 of 10
Mixed Disorders & Special Situations
When electrolytes fall together — refeeding, DKA, massive transfusion, and the dialysis patient.

Refeeding Syndrome

  • At-risk: BMI <16, unintentional weight loss >15% in 3–6 months, negligible intake >10 days, low K⁺/PO₄/Mg²⁺ before feeding
  • Pathophysiology: Insulin surge → intracellular shift of PO₄, K⁺, Mg²⁺ → severe hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia → cardiac failure, arrhythmias, rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure
  • Management: Start feeding at 10–20 kcal/kg/day; check electrolytes daily for first 72 h; replace PO₄, K⁺, Mg²⁺ aggressively; thiamine 200–300 mg IV/PO before feeding

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • Total body deficits: Water ~5–7 L, Na⁺ ~5–10 mmol/kg, K⁺ ~3–5 mmol/kg, PO₄ ~1 mmol/kg, Mg²⁺ ~1 mmol/kg
  • Potassium: Serum K⁺ may be normal or high despite total body depletion — insulin will drive K⁺ intracellularly; replace when K⁺ <5.5 and urine output adequate
  • Phosphate: Routine replacement not recommended unless PO₄ <0.3 or symptomatic (respiratory/cardiac)
  • Bicarbonate: Not recommended unless pH <6.9 (and even then, controversial)

Massive Transfusion

  • Citrate toxicity: Citrate in stored blood chelates calcium → ionised hypocalcaemia → prolonged QT, arrhythmia, hypotension
  • Management: Monitor ionised Ca²⁺; replace calcium gluconate 10–20 mL per 2–4 units transfused if ionised Ca²⁺ <1.0 mmol/L or ECG changes
  • Potassium: Stored blood contains K⁺ (leaks from cells) — hyperkalaemia risk in massive transfusion, especially in renal failure

The Dialysis Patient

  • Potassium: Hyperkalaemia common; manage with dialysis, binders, dietary restriction; avoid succinylcholine (raises K⁺)
  • Calcium: Hypocalcaemia from reduced 1α-hydroxylation; use active vitamin D (alfacalcidol/calcitriol); phosphate binders
  • Magnesium: Hypermagnesaemia possible from reduced excretion — reduce Mg-containing laxatives/antacids
  • Phosphate: Hyperphosphataemia — binders with meals, dietary restriction, dialysis
  • Sodium: Hyponatraemia may reflect volume status or "sick cell" — assess with osmolality and volume

3 · The Top 10 Traps

ODS is devastating and preventable. Limit correction to 6–8 mmol/L/24 h (some sources allow 10). If exceeded, stop active correction and consider desmopressin. The "rule of 6" — 6 mmol/L in 6 hours for severe symptoms, then stop — is a practical safety framework [citation:10].
Calcium is the first drug — it stabilises the myocardium while the other measures take effect. Do not wait for insulin/glucose to work before giving calcium.
Hypomagnesaemia causes renal K⁺ wasting and impairs cellular uptake. Replace Mg first (0.5–1 g/h) — the K⁺ will then respond [citation:14].
Low Mg impairs PTH secretion and action. Refractory hypocalcaemia should prompt a magnesium check and replacement.
In hypoalbuminaemia, total calcium is falsely low. Correct for albumin or measure ionised calcium. The ionised fraction is what matters physiologically.
Adrenal insufficiency mimics SIADH and requires glucocorticoid treatment. Check early morning cortisol before diagnosing SIADH.
Malnourished patients can crash within 24–72 h of feeding. Check PO₄, K⁺, Mg²⁺ daily; start feeding slowly; give thiamine.
Citrate chelates calcium. Monitor ionised Ca²⁺ and replace calcium gluconate if low or if ECG changes develop.
Bicarbonate is not routine for pH >7.1. It causes hypernatraemia, hypercapnia, hypokalaemia, and intracellular acidosis. Reserve for pH <7.1 with instability or specific toxins.
Rapid correction of chronic hypernatraemia causes cerebral oedema. Limit to ≤0.5 mmol/L/h (≤10–12 mmol/L/day). Use the free water deficit formula to plan.

4 · The Synthesis Case

A 58-year-old with small cell lung cancer, confusion, and seizures

Bloods: Na 116 mmol/L, K 3.1, Mg 0.55, corrected Ca 2.05, osmolality 248 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 520, urine Na 65.

Step 1 — Sodium: Severe hyponatraemia (116). Euvolaemic, urine osmolality >100, urine Na >30 → consistent with SIADH. Seizures = symptomatic → needs 3% saline.
Step 2 — Rate: Chronic hyponatraemia → rule of 6. Give 100 mL 3% saline over 10 min, repeat up to 3 times until seizures stop. Target 4–6 mmol/L rise in first 6 h; do not exceed 6–8 mmol/L in 24 h [citation:10].
Step 3 — Potassium: 3.1 (low). But Mg is 0.55 (low). Replace magnesium first (0.5–1 g/h) — potassium will not correct until Mg is repleted [citation:14].
Step 4 — Calcium: Corrected Ca 2.05 (low-normal). Check ionised calcium; correct Mg first (low Mg impairs PTH).
Step 5 — Cause: Small cell lung cancer is a classic cause of SIADH. Treat the cancer; consider tolvaptan if SIADH persists.

Diagnosis: SIADH from small cell lung cancer causing severe symptomatic hyponatraemia, with concurrent hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia. The magnesium is the key to correcting the potassium.
✦ The Final Rule Electrolytes do not exist in isolation. Sodium, potassium, magnesium, and calcium are interlinked. Correcting one without checking the others often fails — or causes harm. Always check the full panel, convert units correctly, correct the rate, and treat the cause.
