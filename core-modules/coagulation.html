The Coagulation Cascade — A Simplified Model

The classical cascade describes intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converging on the common pathway. In vivo, the cell-based model is more accurate — tissue factor initiates coagulation, platelets provide the phospholipid surface, and thrombin generation occurs in overlapping phases.

The Cell-Based Model The classical cascade is a laboratory model. In vivo, coagulation occurs on cell surfaces: initiation on tissue factor-bearing cells, amplification on platelets, and propagation on activated platelet surfaces. This explains why PT and APTT do not always reflect bleeding risk — especially in liver disease and trauma .

⚠ The Conventional Test Trap in Liver Disease In cirrhosis, PT and INR are prolonged because of reduced synthesis of procoagulant factors — but anticoagulant factors (Protein C, antithrombin) are also reduced. The result is a "re-balanced" haemostasis. Prolonged INR does not reliably predict bleeding risk, and correcting it with FFP may cause volume overload and worsen portal hypertension .

Mixing Studies

  • Purpose: Distinguish factor deficiency from inhibitor (e.g., lupus anticoagulant, factor VIII inhibitor)
  • Method: Mix patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma; repeat PT/APTT
  • Correction (normalises): Factor deficiency
  • No correction (remains prolonged): Inhibitor present
  • Partial correction: May indicate a slow-acting inhibitor or factor deficiency with inhibitor

Viscoelastic Testing (TEG/ROTEM)
Whole-blood, real-time assessment of clot formation, strength, and lysis — the tool that conventional tests cannot replace, and the one that requires correct interpretation to guide therapy.

Principles

Viscoelastic testing (VET) measures the viscoelastic properties of whole blood as it clots. A pin or cup oscillates, and the resistance to movement is recorded over time. The result is a graphical representation of clot initiation, amplification, strength, and dissolution .

The FIBTEM Difference FIBTEM contains cytochalasin D, which inhibits platelet GPIIb/IIIa — removing the platelet contribution to clot strength. FIBTEM MCF therefore reflects fibrinogen alone . This is how VET distinguishes platelet deficiency from fibrinogen deficiency: low EXTEM/INTEM MCF with normal FIBTEM → platelets; low EXTEM/INTEM MCF with low FIBTEM → fibrinogen.

Interpretation — Stepwise Approach

  • Step 1 — Prolonged CT/R: Factor deficiency or heparin. If HEPTEM CT corrects → heparin effect; if not → factor deficiency (FFP/PCC).
  • Step 2 — Reduced MCF/MA with normal FIBTEM: Platelet deficiency or dysfunction → platelets.
  • Step 3 — Reduced MCF/MA with low FIBTEM: Fibrinogen deficiency → fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate.
  • Step 4 — Elevated ML/LY30: Hyperfibrinolysis → tranexamic acid.
  • Step 5 — Normal VET but bleeding: Consider surgical bleeding, platelet inhibition (aspirin/clopidogrel), von Willebrand disease, or factor XIII deficiency — VET has limited sensitivity to these .

⚠ What VET Does NOT Detect ROTEM has poor sensitivity to aspirin, clopidogrel, von Willebrand disease, LMWH, and warfarin . A normal VET in a patient on antiplatelet therapy or with vWD does not exclude a bleeding tendency. Combine VET with clinical assessment, medication history, and specific assays where indicated.

Clinical Utility — Evidence

  • Trauma: VET-guided resuscitation improves outcomes and reduces blood product use compared to conventional tests; abnormal EXTEM and FIBTEM clot amplitude and lysis predict mortality .
  • Liver transplantation: ROTEM/TEG algorithms reduce FFP and platelet use while increasing targeted fibrinogen administration .
  • Cirrhosis: VET-guided algorithms reduce unnecessary FFP before invasive procedures .
  • Cardiac surgery: Standardised VET-based algorithms are recommended over clinical discretion and conventional tests .
✦ VET vs Conventional Tests Conventional tests assess quantitative factor levels in plasma and take 30–60 minutes. VET assesses functional clot formation in whole blood, including platelet contribution and fibrinolysis, and provides results in minutes. The correlation between R/CT and PT/INR is weak (r = 0.24–0.37) — VET is not a substitute for factor assays, but a complementary functional assessment .

Inherited Bleeding Disorders
Haemophilia A and B, von Willebrand disease, and the rare factor deficiencies — what the anaesthetist needs to know.

Overview

Haemophilia A and B and von Willebrand disease account for 95–97% of all congenital coagulation disorders. The remaining 3–5% are rare factor deficiencies (factors I, II, V, VII, X, XI, XIII) .

⚠ Neuraxial Anaesthesia in Haemophilia Neuraxial techniques are contraindicated in patients with severe haemophilia or active bleeding unless factor levels are corrected and confirmed. The risk of spinal haematoma is unacceptable. For mild haemophilia, coordinate with haematology; factor levels must be >50% for neuraxial procedures .

Module 05 of 10
Acquired Bleeding Disorders
DIC, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, massive transfusion, and drug-induced coagulopathy.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

  • Definition: Systemic activation of coagulation → widespread fibrin deposition → consumption of platelets and factors → bleeding and thrombosis simultaneously
  • Causes: Sepsis, trauma, obstetric complications (placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism), malignancy, burns, acute pancreatitis
  • Laboratory: Thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer, schistocytes on blood film
  • Diagnosis: ISTH DIC score (platelet count, D-dimer/fibrin markers, PT prolongation, fibrinogen)
  • Management: Treat the underlying cause; support with FFP, platelets, cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen based on bleeding and VET; consider heparin in thrombosis-predominant DIC
⚠ DIC in Obstetrics Obstetric DIC is a life-threatening emergency. The 2024 revised Japanese obstetric DIC score emphasises fibrinogen decline and fibrin-related markers as key diagnostic variables. Early fibrinogen replacement, antithrombin concentrate, and tranexamic acid are recommended alongside interventional haemostasis .

Liver Disease

  • Pathophysiology: Reduced synthesis of procoagulant factors (II, V, VII, IX, X) and anticoagulant factors (Protein C, antithrombin), thrombocytopenia, increased vWF, and rebalanced haemostasis
  • PT/INR: Prolonged — does not predict bleeding risk
  • Fibrinogen: Often low; <1.5 g/L may warrant replacement before major procedures
  • Viscoelastic testing: More accurate than PT/INR for guiding transfusion in cirrhosis
  • Procedures: ROTEM-guided algorithms reduce unnecessary FFP use in cirrhosis

Vitamin K Deficiency

  • Factors affected: II, VII, IX, X (and Proteins C and S)
  • Causes: Malnutrition, malabsorption, antibiotics (gut flora), obstructive jaundice, warfarin
  • PT: Prolonged first (factor VII has shortest half-life)
  • Treatment: Vitamin K 10 mg IV (takes 6–12 h to correct); urgent reversal with PCC or FFP

Massive Transfusion

  • Dilutional coagulopathy: Loss of factors and platelets
  • Citrate toxicity: Ionised hypocalcaemia → impaired coagulation
  • Hypothermia: Impaired enzymatic function
  • Acidosis: Impaired coagulation factor activity
  • Management: 1:1:1 ratio (FFP:platelets:RBC) or VET-guided; calcium replacement; warming

Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)

  • Definition: Arterial or venous thrombosis + pregnancy morbidity + persistent antiphospholipid antibodies
  • Laboratory: Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β₂-glycoprotein I antibodies
  • Paradox: APTT is prolonged in vitro (lupus anticoagulant), but patients are prothrombotic in vivo
  • Perioperative: High thrombotic risk; continue anticoagulation where possible; bridging with LMWH; avoid neuraxial if fully anticoagulated
⚠ Lupus Anticoagulant — The Trap A prolonged APTT in a patient with APS does not mean they are anticoagulated. The lupus anticoagulant interferes with the phospholipid-dependent APTT assay in vitro. The patient is actually prothrombotic. Do not "correct" the APTT with FFP unless there is active bleeding.

⚠ Heparin Resistance Defined as requiring >35,000 units/day to maintain therapeutic APTT. Causes include antithrombin deficiency, increased heparin clearance, and elevated heparin-binding proteins. Treatment: antithrombin concentrate or FFP.

Warfarin

  • Monitoring: INR (target 2.0–3.0 for most indications; 2.5–3.5 for mechanical mitral valves)
  • Mechanism: Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase → reduces synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, X and Proteins C and S
  • Reversal: Vitamin K 5–10 mg IV (slow); PCC (immediate) for major bleeding or urgent surgery; FFP if PCC unavailable
  • Warfarin skin necrosis: Rare complication from early Protein C deficiency; prevent by overlapping heparin when starting warfarin

DOAC Monitoring in Practice Routine monitoring is not required for most patients. However, in emergency surgery, major bleeding, or suspected accumulation (renal impairment, extremes of weight), drug-specific assays or TT/anti-Xa should be used. Standard PT/APTT are unreliable for DOACs.

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)

  • Mechanism: IgG antibodies against platelet factor 4–heparin complexes → platelet activation → thrombocytopenia and thrombosis
  • Timing: 5–10 days after heparin initiation; rapid onset if prior exposure
  • 4Ts Score: Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes — calculate to estimate probability
  • Diagnosis: Intermediate/high 4Ts → immunoassay (PF4 ELISA) → functional assay (serotonin release assay) to confirm
  • Management: Stop heparin immediately; start non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux); avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding
Module 08 of 10
Special Situations
Obstetrics, cardiac surgery, trauma, and the patient on multiple antithrombotics.

Obstetrics

  • Physiological changes: Hypercoagulable state (increased factors VII, VIII, X, fibrinogen; decreased Protein S); increased thrombosis risk
  • Obstetric DIC: Placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, HELLP syndrome, postpartum haemorrhage
  • Neuraxial anaesthesia: Follow ASRA/ESAIC guidelines for anticoagulation; LMWH timing is critical
  • Inherited bleeding disorders in pregnancy: Factor levels may change; coordinate with haematology for delivery planning

Cardiac Surgery

  • Heparinisation: High-dose heparin for cardiopulmonary bypass; monitor ACT (target >400–480 seconds)
  • Protamine reversal: 1 mg per 100 units heparin; risk of protamine reaction (anaphylaxis, pulmonary hypertension)
  • HIT: Complex; alternative anticoagulants (bivalirudin) for CPB in HIT patients
  • Viscoelastic testing: Guides transfusion and reduces re-exploration

Trauma

  • Acute traumatic coagulopathy: Endothelial injury, shock, hyperfibrinolysis
  • Management: Damage control resuscitation; tranexamic acid; VET-guided transfusion; fibrinogen and calcium replacement
  • Viscoelastic testing: Superior to conventional tests for guiding resuscitation in trauma

The Patient on Multiple Antithrombotics

  • Assessment: Bleeding risk vs thrombotic risk; procedure-specific risk; timing of last dose
  • Bridging: LMWH bridging for high-risk patients on warfarin; DOACs usually do not require bridging
  • Neuraxial: Follow ASRA guidelines strictly; document last dose and renal function
  • Emergency surgery: Consider reversal agents (PCC, idarucizumab, andexanet) based on drug and urgency

2 · Diagnostic Algorithm — Prolonged APTT

  • Step 1: Is the patient bleeding?
  • Step 2: Check mixing study
  • Corrects: Factor deficiency (VIII, IX, XI, XII) → factor assays
  • Does not correct: Inhibitor (lupus anticoagulant, factor VIII inhibitor) → specific inhibitor assays
  • Step 3: Check heparin contamination (TT prolonged; anti-Xa)
  • Step 4: Consider lupus anticoagulant (prolonged APTT, no bleeding, thrombosis history)

3 · The Top 10 Traps

Conventional tests miss platelet function, vWF, fibrinolysis, and natural anticoagulants. In liver disease and trauma, PT/APTT are unreliable .
Cirrhosis is a re-balanced haemostatic state. Correcting INR with FFP may cause harm. Use VET if available .
Do not "correct" the APTT with FFP. The patient is prothrombotic, not bleeding.
Stop heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant. Do not simply stop heparin and observe .

4 · The Synthesis Case

A 55-year-old with cirrhosis and sepsis, bleeding from a line site

Bloods: Platelets 65 ×10⁹/L, PT 22 s, INR 1.9, APTT 45 s, fibrinogen 1.1 g/L, D-dimer 8.0 mg/L FEU.

Step 1 — Interpret: Thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer → consistent with DIC on a background of cirrhosis.
Step 2 — Clinical: Is the patient bleeding? Yes (line site). Is there thrombosis? Assess.
Step 3 — VET if available: ROTEM may show prolonged CT, reduced MCF, low fibrinogen — guide targeted replacement.
Step 4 — Management: Treat sepsis (source control, antibiotics). Replace fibrinogen (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate) if <1.5 g/L and bleeding. Platelets if <50 ×10⁹/L and bleeding. FFP only if VET indicates factor deficiency. Avoid routine FFP for INR correction .
Step 5 — Monitor: Repeat VET/coagulation after replacement; reassess bleeding.
✦ The Final Rule Coagulation is a balance — bleeding and thrombosis are two sides of the same coin. Treat the patient, not the number. Use the right test for the right question: conventional tests for factor levels, VET for functional clot assessment, and clinical judgement for everything else.
