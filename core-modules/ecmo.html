Module 01 of 12 · Foundations
What ECMO Is — and Is Not
A therapy defined by its limits. Patient selection, indications, contraindications, and the ethical framework that governs its use.
The Core Definition ECMO is a form of extracorporeal life support that provides prolonged gas exchange (and, in VA configuration, circulatory support) outside the body for days to weeks. It is not a treatment — it is a bridge: to recovery, to transplant, or to a decision. The underlying pathology must be potentially reversible, and the patient must have a reasonable chance of meaningful survival [citation:1].

The Central Principle of Patient Selection

ECMO is considered when a patient has a potentially reversible pathology that is not improving despite maximal conventional intensive care, and without ECMO there is a high risk of mortality [citation:1]. This is a deceptively simple statement that demands difficult judgment: the reversibility must be real, the conventional care must be genuinely maximal, and the risk must be high enough to justify the intervention.

⚠ The Selection Problem ECMO does not create reversibility. It buys time for the body to heal — or for a decision to be made. If the pathology is not reversible (severe brain injury, advanced malignancy, irreversible lung fibrosis), ECMO prolongs dying, not life. The most difficult decisions are not about who can be cannulated — they are about who should not be.

The Nomenclature "VV" and "VA" describe the drainage and return sites — not the organs supported. VV = venous drainage, venous return. VA = venous drainage, arterial return. A third configuration, VVA, provides both respiratory and cardiac support by returning blood to both venous and arterial systems.

Configurations — VV, VA, and Hybrids
The anatomy of extracorporeal support. Each configuration has distinct physiology, indications, and complications.

VV-ECMO — Lung Support Only

  • Drainage: Femoral vein (IVC) or internal jugular (SVC)
  • Return: Internal jugular (SVC/RA) or femoral vein
  • Double-lumen cannula: Single insertion site (right IJ) — drainage from SVC/IVC, return to RA [citation:10]
  • Physiology: ECMO blood mixes with native venous blood in the RA → right ventricle → lungs (variable contribution) → left heart → systemic circulation
  • Key limitation: Recirculation — oxygenated blood from the return cannula is drawn back into the drainage cannula, reducing effective gas exchange
  • Native cardiac function must be adequate — VV-ECMO does not support the heart

VA-ECMO — Heart and Lung Support

  • Drainage: Femoral vein (RA/IVC) — most common
  • Return: Femoral artery (retrograde to aorta) or ascending aorta (central)
  • Physiology: ECMO blood enters the arterial system → competes with native cardiac output → mixing point depends on native LV function
  • Afterload: ECMO flow increases arterial pressure → increased LV afterload → LV may distend if native ejection is inadequate → requires venting (IABP, Impella, surgical vent)
  • Differential hypoxaemia (Harlequin syndrome): Native LV ejecting poorly oxygenated blood into the upper body (coronaries, brain) while ECMO provides oxygenated blood to the lower body — monitor right radial PaO₂
  • Limb ischaemia: Femoral arterial cannulation can compromise distal perfusion — distal perfusion catheter (DPC) recommended [citation:3]
⚠ The Afterload Problem in VA-ECMO VA-ECMO increases left ventricular afterload. If the native LV cannot eject against this increased pressure, the LV distends, subendocardial ischaemia develops, and pulmonary oedema can occur. Venting strategies — IABP, Impella, surgical LV vent — may be required. This is not a complication of ECMO per se, but a consequence of its physiology.

Flow, Sweep Gas, and Gas Exchange

The Two Independent Controls Blood flow (L/min) determines oxygenation — the volume of blood passing through the oxygenator per minute. Sweep gas flow (L/min) determines CO₂ removal — the rate at which gas passes through the oxygenator. These are independent: increasing blood flow improves oxygenation but not necessarily CO₂ clearance; increasing sweep gas improves CO₂ clearance but not oxygenation.

⚠ The ECMO Emergency Checklist Every ECMO centre should have a crisis checklist and a circuit emergency drill. The most common circuit emergencies are air embolism, oxygenator failure, and pump failure. Simulation-based training is mandatory in ELSO-endorsed centres [citation:8].

Circuit Longevity and Exchange

  • Oxygenator lifespan: Typically 7–14 days, but varies with patient factors (thrombosis, inflammation, haemolysis)
  • Indications for oxygenator exchange: Delta-P >80 mmHg, falling gas exchange despite FiO₂ 1.0, visible clot, plasma leak
  • Indications for circuit exchange: Thrombosis, infection (catheter-related bloodstream infection), rupture
  • Prophylactic exchange: Some centres exchange circuits routinely (e.g., every 7–10 days); evidence is limited
  • During exchange: Maintain anticoagulation, prepare new circuit, have blood products available, monitor haemodynamics

Physiology of Extracorporeal Support
What ECMO does to haemodynamics, gas exchange, and the native circulation — and why VV and VA are physiologically distinct.

Haemodynamic Effects

ParameterVV-ECMOVA-ECMO
Cardiac outputUnchanged (native CO + ECMO flow)Native CO + ECMO flow — but native CO may fall due to increased afterload
AfterloadUnchangedIncreased — LV must eject against ECMO pressure
PulsatilityPreserved (native heart)May be reduced — depends on native ejection
Right-sided pressuresReduced (drainage from RA/IVC)Reduced (drainage from RA/IVC)
LV distension riskLowHigh — requires venting if native ejection inadequate
Coronary oxygenationDepends on native lungsDepends on mixing — right radial PaO₂ reflects coronary/cerebral oxygenation

Gas Exchange Physiology

ECMO Oxygen Delivery DO₂ = ECMO flow × (CaO₂_ECMO − CvO₂_drainage) × 10
ECMO flow is the primary determinant of oxygen delivery in VV-ECMO. In VA-ECMO, native CO also contributes.
  • Oxygenation: Determined by ECMO blood flow and the oxygenator's gas exchange capacity. In VV-ECMO, the native lungs also contribute — the proportion depends on the degree of lung injury.
  • CO₂ removal: Determined by sweep gas flow. High sweep gas → more CO₂ removed → lower PaCO₂.
  • Recirculation (VV): Oxygenated blood from the return cannula is drawn back into the drainage cannula — reduces effective gas exchange. Fraction of recirculation = (return cannula saturation − drainage cannula saturation) / (return cannula saturation − pre-ECMO venous saturation).
  • Shunt (VA): Native blood from the LV mixes with ECMO blood in the aorta — the mixing point determines regional oxygenation.

The CO₂ Problem — Avoid Rapid Correction

⚠ Rapid CO₂ Reduction in VV-ECMO When VV-ECMO is initiated in a patient with severe hypercapnia, rapid CO₂ removal (via high sweep gas) can cause a sudden fall in PaCO₂. This alters cerebrovascular tone and has been associated with seizures, intracranial haemorrhage, and brain death [citation:6]. ELSO guidelines recommend starting sweep gas at 2 L/min and titrating to allow gradual correction of CO₂ — avoid reducing PaCO₂ by >50% in the first 24 hours [citation:6].

Native Cardiac Function During ECMO

  • VV-ECMO: Native CO must be adequate. If cardiac function deteriorates (e.g., sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy, right heart failure), VV-ECMO alone may be insufficient — consider VA-ECMO or VVA-ECMO.
  • VA-ECMO: Native CO may be zero (no ejection) or may compete with ECMO flow. The balance between native and ECMO flow determines the mixing point and regional oxygenation.
  • Right heart failure: Common in ARDS (cor pulmonale) — VV-ECMO can improve right heart function by correcting hypoxaemia and hypercapnia [citation:6].
  • LV recovery: In VA-ECMO, native ejection may return as the heart recovers — monitor with echocardiography and arterial waveform pulsatility.

Cannula Sizing

  • Drainage cannula: Larger is better for flow — typically 21–29 Fr. Undersized drainage cannula limits flow and causes negative pressure (chugging).
  • Return cannula: Sized to match required flow — 15–21 Fr for arterial (VA); 19–25 Fr for venous (VV).
  • VA-ECMO arterial cannula: ELSO suggests up to 21 Fr for patients requiring more support [citation:3]. Smaller cannulae (15–17 Fr) may be adequate for lower flow requirements and reduce limb ischaemia risk.
  • Distal perfusion catheter (DPC): A 6–8 Fr introducer sheath or paediatric arterial cannula placed distal to the arterial cannula to perfuse the limb [citation:3]. Limb ischaemia during VA-ECMO has been reported in 10–70% of patients [citation:3].
The Distal Perfusion Catheter Femoral arterial cannulation can obstruct flow to the ipsilateral limb. A distal perfusion catheter (DPC) — placed in the superficial femoral artery distal to the cannula — provides antegrade flow to the limb. ELSO recommends a wire-reinforced 6–8 Fr introducer sheath as a DPC, though it may not be needed with smaller arterial cannulae (15–17 Fr) [citation:3]. Recent evidence suggests a paediatric arterial cannula as DPC may provide better flow than an introducer sheath [citation:3].

Cannulation in Special Situations

  • ECPR (cardiac arrest): Femoral cannulation is standard; ultrasound or landmark technique; minimal interruption of CPR
  • Post-cardiotomy: Central cannulation may be possible (chest already open); peripheral cannulation if chest closed
  • Awake ECMO: Femoro-jugular or dual-lumen; allows mobilisation and rehabilitation
  • Difficult anatomy: Ultrasound guidance is essential; consider alternative sites (axillary, cervical)

Anticoagulation & Haemostasis
The perpetual balance — preventing circuit thrombosis while avoiding bleeding. A moving target that defines ECMO management.
The ECMO Paradox The circuit is thrombogenic — blood contacts artificial surfaces, and stasis in the oxygenator promotes clot formation. Anticoagulation is required. But the patient is often critically ill, coagulopathic, and at risk of bleeding. Every ECMO patient is managed on a sliding scale between thrombosis and haemorrhage.

⚠ The Bleeding-Thrombosis Balance A recent observational study (HEART-ECMO) found that withholding the heparin bolus at cannulation resulted in comparable aPTT values, less major bleeding (42.9% vs 25.0%), and similar thrombotic events [citation:4]. This suggests that a limited heparin exposure strategy may be safe — but this is observational and requires RCT validation. The optimal anticoagulation strategy remains uncertain.

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)

  • Mechanism: IgG antibodies against heparin-PF4 complexes → platelet activation → thrombosis (not just bleeding)
  • Recognition: Falling platelets (>50% from baseline) 5–10 days after heparin exposure; thrombosis despite heparin
  • Diagnosis: 4Ts score; anti-PF4 antibody assay; serotonin release assay (confirmatory)
  • Management: Stop heparin immediately. Use alternative anticoagulants: argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor), bivalirudin, danaparoid. Do not use LMWH (cross-reactivity).
  • ECMO circuit: Argatroban or bivalirudin can be used for circuit anticoagulation — requires monitoring with aPTT or specific assays.

Bleeding Management

  • Assessment: Identify source (cannulation site, surgical site, GI, intracranial, pulmonary)
  • Anticoagulation: Reduce or temporarily hold heparin; consider reversal with protamine (for heparin)
  • Blood products: Platelets (if <50,000 or bleeding with platelet dysfunction), FFP (if coagulopathic), cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen <150), RBC (target Hb 7–9 g/dL)
  • Point-of-care testing: TEG/ROTEM to guide targeted transfusion
  • Surgical/interventional: Embolisation, packing, surgical exploration
  • Intracranial haemorrhage: Emergency neurosurgical consultation; reverse anticoagulation; consider ECMO weaning/decannulation if survival unlikely

Neurological Complications

  • Intracranial haemorrhage: 2–5%; associated with anticoagulation, rapid CO₂ changes, hypertension, cerebral autoregulation loss
  • Ischaemic stroke: 2–5%; emboli from circuit, native heart, or cannula
  • Seizures: 5–10%; associated with rapid CO₂ changes, hypoperfusion, emboli [citation:6]
  • Delirium: Common; multifactorial (inflammation, sleep deprivation, sedation)
  • Brain death: Rare but catastrophic; associated with rapid CO₂ correction in severe hypercapnia [citation:6]
⚠ Rapid CO₂ Correction and Neurological Injury In VV-ECMO for severe hypercapnia, rapid CO₂ removal can cause cerebral vasoconstriction, ischaemia, and haemorrhage. Start sweep gas low (2 L/min) and titrate slowly — avoid reducing PaCO₂ by >50% in the first 24 hours [citation:6]. This is one of the most important safety lessons in ECMO.

Renal Complications

  • Acute kidney injury (AKI): 40–70% in VA-ECMO; 50–75% in VV-ECMO [citation:2]
  • Mechanisms: Hypoperfusion, inflammation, haemolysis (free haemoglobin), emboli, nephrotoxins
  • Renal replacement therapy (RRT): Required in 40–50% of patients with AKI [citation:2]
  • ECMO-CRRT connection: Can connect CRRT to ECMO circuit (post-oxygenator, pre-pump) — requires careful pressure management
  • Prognosis: AKI is associated with increased mortality; recovery is possible but may be prolonged

Infectious Complications

  • Catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI): 5–10%; cannula site, circuit
  • Ventilator-associated pneumonia: Common in VV-ECMO patients
    • Bloodstream infection: 10–20%; associated with cannula duration, circuit exchange
  • Prevention: Aseptic technique, daily review of cannula necessity, surveillance cultures, circuit exchange if infected

Other Complications

  • Haemolysis: From pump shear stress, oxygenator, cannula — monitor free haemoglobin, LDH, haptoglobin
  • Differential hypoxaemia (Harlequin syndrome): VA-ECMO — native LV ejecting deoxygenated blood to upper body; monitor right radial PaO₂
  • LV distension: VA-ECMO — requires venting (IABP, Impella, surgical vent)
  • Acquired von Willebrand syndrome: Loss of high-molecular-weight vWF multimers → bleeding; associated with high shear stress
  • Nutritional depletion: Catabolism, poor enteral intake; consider parenteral nutrition if needed

VV-ECMO — Respiratory Support
The six phases of prolonged VV support — from ultra-lung-protective ventilation to liberation. A conceptual framework for the ICU course.
The Six Phases of Prolonged VV-ECMO A 2026 conceptual framework describes VV-ECMO support in six phases: (1) Ultra-lung-protective ventilation; (2) Stabilisation; (3) Recovery; (4) Weaning; (5) Liberation trial; (6) Decannulation [citation:6]. Each phase has distinct priorities and risks.

Phase 1 — Ultra-Lung-Protective Ventilation

  • Goal: Prevent further ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) in the highly inflamed lung [citation:6]
  • Ventilator settings: Low tidal volume (4–6 mL/kg IBW), low plateau pressure (<25 cmH₂O), low driving pressure (<15 cmH₂O), PEEP to maintain recruitment
  • ECMO support: High blood flow (>5 L/min) to support gas exchange and allow minimal ventilator support [citation:6]
  • Avoid: Routine recruitment manoeuvres — no demonstrated benefit, risk of barotrauma [citation:6]
  • CO₂ management: Start sweep gas low (2 L/min); gradual correction of hypercapnia [citation:6]

Phase 2 — Stabilisation

  • Haemodynamic support: Fluids, vasopressors, inotropes as needed
  • Right heart function: VV-ECMO corrects hypoxaemia and hypercapnia → reduces pulmonary vasoconstriction → improves right heart function [citation:6]
  • Sedation: Minimise — allow awakening and mobilisation if possible
  • Nutrition: Enteral nutrition early; parenteral if not tolerated
  • Monitoring: Daily echocardiography, ABG, chest X-ray, inflammatory markers

Phase 3 — Recovery

  • Lung recovery: Improvement in compliance, gas exchange, radiology
  • ECMO weaning: Gradually reduce sweep gas and blood flow as native lung function improves
  • Ventilator weaning: Increase tidal volume, reduce PEEP, assess spontaneous breathing
  • Mobilisation: Awake ECMO allows physical rehabilitation — important for long-term outcomes

Phase 4–6 — Weaning, Liberation Trial, Decannulation

  • Weaning: Progressive reduction in sweep gas flow (SGF) and ECMO blood flow over several days [citation:6]
  • Liberation trial: Sweep gas off trial (SGOT) — SGF turned off for a variable observation period; assess native gas exchange [citation:6]
  • Unsafe liberation: Death, re-cannulation, or significant deterioration within 48 hours of decannulation [citation:6]
  • Predictors of unsafe weaning: Elevated ventilatory ratio during SGOT; high PaCO₂; high respiratory rate [citation:14]
  • Decannulation: Remove cannulae after successful liberation trial; monitor for deterioration

Cardiogenic shock, post-cardiotomy support, and the haemodynamic consequences of extracorporeal circulation.

Haemodynamic Management on VA-ECMO

  • Flow: 3–6 L/min; titrated to MAP >65, lactate clearance, end-organ perfusion
  • Afterload: Increased by ECMO flow — monitor for LV distension (echocardiography, arterial waveform pulsatility)
  • LV venting: IABP, Impella, surgical vent if LV distension or pulmonary oedema
  • Inotropes: May be needed to maintain native ejection (prevents stasis and thrombosis)
  • Differential hypoxaemia: Monitor right radial PaO₂ — if low, increase ECMO flow or consider VVA-ECMO
  • Haemolysis: Monitor free Hb, LDH — optimise cannula position, reduce RPM if possible
⚠ The LV Distension Problem VA-ECMO increases afterload. If the native LV cannot eject, blood stagnates in the LV → distension → subendocardial ischaemia → pulmonary oedema → worsening cardiac function. Venting is essential if native ejection is inadequate. Monitor with echocardiography (LV dimensions, aortic valve opening) and arterial waveform pulsatility.

Post-Cardiotomy VA-ECMO

  • Incidence: 0.5–2% of cardiac surgery; mortality 60–70% [citation:7]
  • Indications: Failure to wean from CPB, post-operative cardiac arrest, refractory shock
  • Cannulation: Central (chest open) or peripheral (femoral)
  • Complications: Bleeding (most common), multi-organ failure, neurological injury [citation:7]
  • Left ventricular unloading: IABP used in ~47% of post-cardiotomy ECMO patients [citation:7]
  • Outcomes: In-hospital mortality ~69%; one-year survival ~30% [citation:7]

ECPR — Extracorporeal CPR
The most time-critical application of ECMO — and the one with the narrowest window for benefit.
The ECPR Window ECPR is the initiation of ECMO during cardiac arrest refractory to conventional CPR. The benefit is time-dependent: the shorter the low-flow time (arrest to ECMO flow), the better the neurological outcome. Beyond 20–30 minutes of conventional CPR, the probability of meaningful survival falls precipitously.

ECPR Outcomes

  • Survival to discharge: 20–40% in selected patients (witnessed, shockable, short low-flow time)
  • Neurological outcome: Favourable in ~70–80% of survivors
  • Predictors of poor outcome: Prolonged low-flow time, non-shockable rhythm, high lactate, renal failure, neurological injury
  • ELSO registry: ECPR survival ~30% overall — highly dependent on selection and centre experience
⚠ The ECPR Decision ECPR is resource-intensive and requires a team available 24/7. The decision to initiate must be made rapidly, often with incomplete information. The most important predictors of benefit are witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, shockable rhythm, and short low-flow time. If these are absent, the probability of meaningful survival is low.

Weaning Trials

  • VV-ECMO — Sweep Gas Off Trial (SGOT): Turn off sweep gas; observe for 1–4 hours; assess PaCO₂, pH, respiratory rate, work of breathing [citation:6]
  • Predictors of SGOT failure: Elevated ventilatory ratio, high PaCO₂, high respiratory rate, increased work of breathing [citation:14]
  • VA-ECMO — Flow reduction trial: Reduce ECMO flow to 1–1.5 L/min; assess MAP, cardiac output, echocardiography, lactate
  • Trial failure: Deterioration in gas exchange, haemodynamics, or clinical status → resume full support
  • Unsafe liberation: Death, re-cannulation, or significant deterioration within 48 hours of decannulation [citation:6]

Decannulation

  • VV-ECMO: Remove cannulae after successful SGOT; monitor for respiratory deterioration; consider high-flow nasal cannula or NIV
  • VA-ECMO: Remove cannulae after successful flow reduction trial; monitor for haemodynamic deterioration; maintain inotropes/vasopressors if needed
  • Post-decannulation: Monitor cannulation site for bleeding; assess limb perfusion (if femoral); continue anticoagulation if indicated
  • Rehabilitation: Early mobilisation; respiratory physiotherapy; nutritional support
✦ The Goal of Weaning Weaning is not a race. The goal is safe liberation — not the earliest possible decannulation. A failed trial prolongs ECMO duration and increases complications. Patience, protocolised assessment, and daily readiness evaluation are the keys.
