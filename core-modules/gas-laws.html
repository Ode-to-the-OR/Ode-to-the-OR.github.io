Module 01 of 10
The Ideal Gas Laws Boyle · Charles · Gay-Lussac · Avogadro
The foundation of respiratory physiology and anaesthetic equipment. Every gas in the operating theatre obeys these laws.
The Ideal Gas Equation PV = nRT — where P = pressure, V = volume, n = number of moles, R = universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K), T = absolute temperature (Kelvin).

Boyle's Law — Pressure & Volume

Boyle's Law (Isothermal) P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
At constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

Clinical relevance:

  • Respiration: Diaphragmatic descent increases thoracic volume → decreases intrathoracic pressure → air flows in. Exhalation is the reverse.
  • Laryngoscopy: Compression of the chest during difficult intubation can expel gas from the lungs.
  • Anaesthetic circuit: Compression of the reservoir bag during manual ventilation — pressure increases as volume decreases.
  • Pneumothorax: A tension pneumothorax worsens as intrathoracic volume decreases (or pressure increases) — needle decompression is Boyle's law in action.
  • Gas cylinders: As a cylinder empties, pressure falls — the remaining gas expands to fill the cylinder volume.
Boyle's Law in the OR When you squeeze the reservoir bag, you reduce its volume and increase the pressure inside — driving gas into the patient's lungs. When you release, the bag expands, pressure drops, and gas flows in from the fresh gas supply.

Charles's Law — Temperature & Volume

Charles's Law (Isobaric) V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
At constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature (Kelvin).

Clinical relevance:

  • Heated humidifiers: As gas is warmed to body temperature, its volume increases — this is why heated circuits must accommodate expansion.
  • Body plethysmography: Measures thoracic gas volume by warming and humidifying inhaled gas.
  • Liquid oxygen: Stored at −183°C; as it warms to room temperature, it expands ~860× — a small liquid volume becomes a large gas volume.
  • Nitrous oxide cylinders: N₂O is stored as a liquid at room temperature — as gas is withdrawn, the liquid vaporises, maintaining pressure until the liquid is exhausted.
⚠ The Temperature Trap A gas cylinder left in the sun can build dangerous pressure. Always store cylinders below 50°C, away from direct sunlight and heat sources. The same applies to the anaesthetic machine's internal components.

Gay-Lussac's Law — Pressure & Temperature

Gay-Lussac's Law (Isochoric) P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂
At constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

Clinical relevance:

  • Gas cylinders in hot environments: A cylinder at 20°C with 200 bar pressure will reach ~220 bar at 40°C — a dangerous rise.
  • Autoclaving of equipment: Sealed containers experience pressure increases with temperature.
  • Endotracheal tube cuffs: In a patient with fever, the cuff pressure increases — hence the need for pressure monitoring.
  • Laryngeal mask airways: Cuff pressure rises with temperature — monitor and adjust.

Avogadro's Law — Volume & Moles

Avogadro's Law V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂
Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

Clinical relevance:

  • Molar volume: At STP (0°C, 1 atm), 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L. This allows calculation of gas volumes from molar quantities.
  • Oxygen consumption: VO₂ is often expressed in mL/min — at STP, this can be converted to moles.
  • CO₂ production: The respiratory quotient (RQ = VCO₂/VO₂) reflects metabolic substrate utilisation.
  • Anaesthetic vaporisers: The amount of vapour delivered depends on the number of moles of liquid anaesthetic vaporised.

The Combined Gas Law

Combining Boyle, Charles & Gay-Lussac P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂
Describes the behaviour of a fixed amount of gas when pressure, volume, and temperature all change.

Clinical application: When gas moves from the anaesthetic machine (room temperature, atmospheric pressure) to the alveoli (body temperature, slightly negative pressure), all three variables change. The combined gas law allows us to calculate the volume and pressure at each step.

STP vs BTPS STP: Standard Temperature and Pressure — 0°C (273.15 K), 1 atm (760 mmHg). BTPS: Body Temperature and Pressure, Saturated — 37°C (310.15 K), 1 atm, fully humidified. Gas volumes measured at STP must be converted to BTPS for physiological calculations. The conversion factor is approximately 1.21.
✦ The Anaesthetist's Rule of Thumb When you compress a gas, it heats up. When you expand it, it cools. This is why rapid decompression of a gas cylinder can cause frost on the valve — and why rebreathing circuits must be designed to handle the heat and moisture from exhaled gas.
Module 02 of 10
Dalton's Law & Henry's Law Partial Pressures & Solubility
The two laws that govern gas exchange in the lung and the behaviour of volatile anaesthetics.

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

Dalton's Law Ptotal = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ... + Pn
In a mixture of non-reacting gases, the total pressure equals the sum of the partial pressures of each gas.

Each gas in a mixture behaves as if it alone occupied the entire volume. The partial pressure of a gas is the pressure it would exert if it were the only gas present.

Clinical Applications of Dalton's Law

  • Alveolar gas composition: The PO₂, PCO₂, and PN₂ in the alveoli each contribute to total alveolar pressure. PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) − PaCO₂/R.
  • Humidification: Water vapour (PH₂O = 47 mmHg at 37°C) takes up part of the total pressure, reducing the partial pressure available for oxygen.
  • Nitrous oxide: When N₂O is added to the inspired gas, it displaces nitrogen and reduces the partial pressure of oxygen — this is why FiO₂ must be increased when using N₂O.
  • Anaesthetic monitoring: The gas analyser measures partial pressures of each agent, which sum to the total pressure (typically atmospheric in a non-pressurised circuit).
  • Hyperbaric therapy: At 3 atm, the total pressure is tripled, and each gas's partial pressure is tripled — the basis of hyperbaric oxygen therapy.
Alveolar Gas Equation — Dalton's Law Applied PAO₂ = PIO₂ − (PaCO₂ / R)
PIO₂ = FiO₂ × (PB − PH₂O) · R = respiratory quotient (~0.8)
Nitrous Oxide & Dalton's Law N₂O (MAC 105%) is a potent analgesic and anaesthetic adjuvant. When 70% N₂O is used, the remaining 30% must contain enough oxygen — typically 30% O₂ + 70% N₂O. If you add 70% N₂O to air (21% O₂), the oxygen partial pressure falls dramatically because N₂O has displaced nitrogen and some oxygen. Always calculate the true FiO₂ when using N₂O.

Henry's Law of Gas Solubility

Henry's Law C = k × Pgas
The amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid. k = solubility coefficient (varies with gas, liquid, and temperature).

Key concepts:

  • Solubility coefficient: The volume of gas (at STP) dissolved per unit volume of liquid at a partial pressure of 1 atm. For blood at 37°C: O₂ = 0.024, CO₂ = 0.48, N₂ = 0.012.
  • Blood–gas partition coefficient: The ratio of the concentration of gas in blood to that in the gas phase at equilibrium. This determines the uptake and distribution of volatile anaesthetics.
  • Temperature: Gas solubility decreases as temperature increases — this is why warming a liquid reduces its ability to hold dissolved gas.

Clinical Applications of Henry's Law

  • Decompression sickness ("the bends"): Nitrogen dissolved in blood and tissues at high pressure comes out of solution when pressure is reduced too rapidly — forming bubbles. Divers must decompress slowly.
  • Anaesthetic uptake: The blood–gas partition coefficient determines how quickly an anaesthetic equilibrates between alveoli and blood. A low blood–gas coefficient (e.g., desflurane, 0.42) means rapid equilibration and fast induction/recovery. A high coefficient (e.g., halothane, 2.4) means slow equilibration and slower induction/recovery.
  • Nitrous oxide diffusion: N₂O has a low blood–gas coefficient (0.47) — it enters and leaves the blood rapidly. This is why N₂O is used for rapid induction and emergence.
  • Gas embolism: Air bubbles in the bloodstream follow Henry's law — they expand as pressure falls (e.g., during ascent) and can cause obstruction.
  • Cardiopulmonary bypass: Oxygenators rely on Henry's law to dissolve oxygen in the blood — the partial pressure of oxygen in the gas phase determines the dissolved oxygen content.
⚠ Nitrous Oxide & Air-filled Spaces N₂O is 34× more soluble in blood than nitrogen. When N₂O enters an air-filled space (pneumothorax, bowel, middle ear, endotracheal tube cuff, air embolism), it diffuses in faster than nitrogen diffuses out — the space expands. This can be catastrophic. Avoid N₂O in: pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle ear surgery, retinal surgery, air embolism, and in patients with air-filled endotracheal tube cuffs (use saline-filled cuffs).
✦ The Second Gas Effect When a high concentration of a soluble gas (e.g., N₂O) is administered, it rapidly diffuses from alveoli into blood, reducing alveolar volume. This concentrates the other gases present (e.g., volatile anaesthetic), increasing their alveolar partial pressure and accelerating their uptake. This is the concentration effect and second gas effect — both direct consequences of Henry's law and gas diffusion.
Module 03 of 10
Fick's Law & Graham's Law Diffusion & Effusion
How gases move across membranes and through small openings — from alveoli to capillaries to anaesthetic circuits.

Fick's Law of Diffusion

Fick's Law V̇gas = (A × D × ΔP) / T
V̇ = rate of diffusion · A = surface area · D = diffusion coefficient · ΔP = partial pressure gradient · T = membrane thickness

The diffusion coefficient (D) depends on:

  • Solubility of the gas in the membrane (directly proportional)
  • Molecular weight of the gas (inversely proportional to the square root)
Diffusion Coefficient D ∝ Solubility / √MW
CO₂ diffuses ~20× faster than O₂ across the alveolar membrane — despite a smaller partial pressure gradient.

Clinical Applications of Fick's Law

  • Alveolar gas exchange: O₂ and CO₂ diffuse across the alveolar–capillary membrane. CO₂ is more soluble, so it diffuses rapidly despite a smaller gradient. O₂ is less soluble but has a larger gradient.
  • Diffusion limitation: In interstitial lung disease, membrane thickening (↑T) reduces diffusion. In emphysema, surface area (A) is reduced.
  • Exercise: Increased cardiac output reduces capillary transit time — if equilibration is incomplete, diffusion limitation occurs.
  • Anaesthetic uptake: Volatile anaesthetics cross the alveolar–capillary membrane by diffusion. Their blood–gas partition coefficient (related to solubility) determines the rate of uptake.
  • Pulse oximetry: Light diffusion through tissue follows similar principles.
  • Dermal absorption: Fentanyl patches, local anaesthetic creams — diffusion across skin follows Fick's law.
Fick's Law & CO₂ Retention CO₂ diffuses ~20× faster than O₂. In diseases with alveolar–capillary membrane thickening (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis), O₂ diffusion is impaired first — leading to hypoxaemia with relative preservation of CO₂ clearance. Hypercapnia develops later, when the membrane is severely thickened or when ventilation is inadequate.

Graham's Law of Effusion

Graham's Law Rate₁ / Rate₂ = √(MW₂ / MW₁)
The rate of effusion (or diffusion) of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molecular weight.

Clinical applications:

  • Heliox (70% helium / 30% oxygen): Helium has a low molecular weight (4 g/mol), so heliox flows more easily through narrow airways than air or oxygen. It is used in upper airway obstruction (tumour, croup, post-extubation stridor) to reduce turbulent flow and work of breathing.
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O) vs. nitrogen: N₂O (MW 44) is heavier than nitrogen (MW 28), but its solubility dominates its diffusion behaviour — the blood–gas partition coefficient is the key factor for anaesthetic uptake.
  • Carbon dioxide elimination: CO₂ (MW 44) diffuses rapidly despite its higher molecular weight because of its high solubility.
  • Gas analyser response times: Low-molecular-weight gases diffuse faster, affecting the response time of gas analysers.
Heliox — The Low-Density Gas Heliox (typically 70:30 He:O₂) reduces Reynolds number by lowering gas density, converting turbulent flow to laminar flow in narrowed airways. This reduces resistance and work of breathing. It is particularly useful in upper airway obstruction, asthma, and post-extubation stridor. Note: heliox requires a special flowmeter because standard flowmeters are calibrated for air/oxygen.
✦ Diffusion & Anaesthetic Induction The rate of anaesthetic induction depends on the alveolar–arterial partial pressure gradient. A high inspired concentration, high alveolar ventilation, and low blood solubility all increase the gradient and speed induction. Fick's law governs the transfer from alveoli to blood, and from blood to tissues.
Module 04 of 10
Flow & Poiseuille's Law Resistance in Airways & Circuits
The physics of gas flow through tubes — from endotracheal tubes to bronchioles to breathing circuits.

Poiseuille's Law (Laminar Flow)

Poiseuille's Law Q̇ = (π × r⁴ × ΔP) / (8 × η × L)
Q̇ = flow · r = radius · ΔP = pressure gradient · η = viscosity · L = length

The critical insight: Flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius. A 50% reduction in radius reduces flow to 1/16th of its original value — a 94% reduction!

Clinical Applications

  • Endotracheal tube resistance: A 7.0 mm ETT has ~16× the resistance of an 8.0 mm ETT (because resistance ∝ 1/r⁴). This is why smaller tubes increase work of breathing.
  • Bronchospasm: A 20% reduction in airway radius increases resistance by 2.4× (1/0.8⁴ = 2.44). This is why even mild bronchospasm significantly increases work of breathing.
  • Airway secretions: Mucus plugs reduce effective radius — dramatically increasing resistance.
  • Breathing circuit tubing: Wider, shorter tubing reduces resistance. Corrugated tubing increases resistance slightly due to turbulent flow.
  • Nasal cannula: Flow is limited by the narrow nasal passages — a key factor in determining the effective FiO₂.
  • Suction catheters: Smaller catheters generate higher negative pressures and are less effective at clearing secretions.
⚠ The Fourth Power Rule Because resistance ∝ 1/r⁴, small changes in airway calibre have massive effects on resistance. A 10% reduction in radius increases resistance by 52%. A 20% reduction increases it by 144%. This is why even mild airway oedema or bronchospasm can cause significant respiratory distress.

Turbulent Flow & Reynolds Number

Reynolds Number Re = (ρ × v × d) / η
ρ = density · v = velocity · d = diameter · η = viscosity · Re > 2000 → turbulent flow

Turbulent flow occurs when:

  • Flow velocity is high (e.g., peak inspiratory flow, coughing)
  • Gas density is high (e.g., heliox reduces density and promotes laminar flow)
  • Airway diameter is large (trachea — turbulent even at rest)
  • Viscosity is low

Clinical relevance:

  • Heliox: Low density (ρ) reduces Reynolds number, converting turbulent to laminar flow — reducing resistance.
  • Upper airway obstruction: High-velocity flow through a narrowed orifice is turbulent — heliox helps.
  • Anaesthetic circuits: Turbulent flow in narrow tubing increases resistance to breathing.
  • Auscultation: Breath sounds are generated by turbulent flow in the large airways.

The Anaesthetist's Flow Dilemma During spontaneous ventilation, peak inspiratory flow rates can reach 30–60 L/min — high enough to generate turbulent flow in the ETT. This is why work of breathing increases with smaller ETTs. During positive pressure ventilation, flow is typically lower and more laminar, but high peak flows can still occur.
✦ Clinical Pearl — ETT Size Matters A 0.5 mm decrease in ETT internal diameter increases resistance by ~40%. This is why extubating to a smaller tube in a patient with upper airway swelling can precipitate respiratory failure. Always choose the largest ETT that can be safely passed.

Bernoulli's Principle & The Venturi Effect Pressure, Velocity & Entrainment
How changes in flow velocity create pressure differences — the physics behind nebulisers, Venturi masks, and jet ventilation.

Bernoulli's Principle

Bernoulli's Equation P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
P = pressure · ρ = density · v = velocity · g = gravity · h = height

Key concept: As the velocity of a fluid increases, its pressure decreases. This is the principle behind the Venturi effect.

Clinical Applications of Bernoulli's Principle

  • Venturi masks: Oxygen flows through a narrow orifice, creating a high-velocity jet that entrains room air — delivering a precise FiO₂.
  • Nebulisers: High-velocity gas flow creates a pressure drop that draws liquid up a tube (Venturi effect) and atomises it into fine particles.
  • Jet ventilation: High-pressure gas is delivered through a narrow cannula — the Venturi effect entrains additional gas, increasing delivered volume.
  • Aneurysms: As blood flows through a widened vessel, velocity decreases and pressure increases — promoting further dilation and rupture.
  • Atherosclerosis: Plaque narrows the artery, increasing velocity and decreasing pressure — contributing to endothelial dysfunction.
  • Pulmonary artery catheterisation: The balloon flotation catheter uses Bernoulli's principle to wedge in the pulmonary artery.
  • Ventilator flow sensors: Pressure differences across a fixed resistance (pneumotachograph) are used to measure flow.

The Venturi Effect

Venturi Effect A fluid flowing through a constricted section of a tube increases in velocity and decreases in pressure, creating suction that entrains another fluid.
This is the principle behind entrainment devices — Venturi masks, nebulisers, jet ventilators, and suction devices.

Why Venturi Masks Are Used Venturi masks deliver a precise, known FiO₂ independent of the patient's inspiratory flow — because the total flow (O₂ + entrained air) exceeds the patient's peak inspiratory flow. This is ideal for patients with COPD, where controlled oxygen therapy is critical (target SpO₂ 88–92%).

Nebulisers

  • Jet nebuliser: Compressed gas (air or O₂) passes through a narrow orifice, creating a Venturi effect that draws liquid up from the reservoir. The liquid is shattered into fine droplets by the high-velocity gas stream.
  • Ultrasonic nebuliser: Uses high-frequency vibrations to create droplets — no gas flow required, so no Venturi effect.
  • Mesh nebuliser: Uses a vibrating mesh to extrude liquid through tiny holes — the most efficient nebuliser type.
  • Particle size: Optimal for alveolar deposition is 1–5 μm. Larger particles deposit in the upper airway; smaller particles are exhaled.

Jet Ventilation & Venturi Entrainment

  • Manual jet ventilation: A high-pressure gas source (e.g., 50 psi) is connected to a small-bore cannula. The Venturi effect entrains additional air, increasing the delivered volume.
  • High-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV): Used in laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, and airway surgery — delivers small tidal volumes at high frequencies.
  • Venturi principle in suction: The same physics that drives entrainment also explains how suction devices work — high-velocity gas creates a negative pressure that draws fluid.
⚠ Venturi Effect & Barotrauma In jet ventilation, if the expiratory path is obstructed, gas can be trapped — leading to barotrauma. Always ensure a clear expiratory pathway and monitor airway pressures. The Venturi effect can also entrain air during jet ventilation, potentially diluting the FiO₂.
✦ The Anaesthetist's Venturi Toolkit Venturi masks for controlled oxygen, jet nebulisers for bronchodilators, jet ventilation for shared airway surgery, and suction for airway clearance — all rely on the same principle. Understanding Bernoulli and Venturi helps you troubleshoot these devices when they fail.
Module 06 of 10
Laplace's Law & Alveolar Stability Surface Tension & Surfactant
Why small alveoli don't collapse into large ones — and what happens when surfactant fails.

Laplace's Law

Laplace's Law P = (2 × T) / r
P = pressure · T = surface tension · r = radius

Key insight: For a given surface tension, the pressure inside a smaller alveolus is higher than in a larger alveolus. Without surfactant, small alveoli would empty into large alveoli — a process called alveolar instability.

The Problem of Alveolar Instability

  • If two alveoli of different sizes are connected, the smaller one has higher pressure (P ∝ 1/r) and would empty into the larger one.
  • This would lead to progressive collapse of small alveoli and overdistension of large ones — catastrophic for gas exchange.
  • Surfactant solves this problem by reducing surface tension more in small alveoli than in large ones — equalising pressures and stabilising the lung.

Surfactant — The Biological Solution

  • Composition: ~90% lipids (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, DPPC), ~10% proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D).
  • Produced by: Type II pneumocytes.
  • Function: Reduces surface tension, preventing alveolar collapse at end-expiration, reducing work of breathing, and maintaining alveolar stability.
  • Laplace's law with surfactant: Surface tension (T) varies with alveolar radius — lower in small alveoli, higher in large alveoli. This equalises pressures and stabilises the lung.

Clinical Applications of Laplace's Law

  • Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS): Surfactant deficiency in premature infants → alveolar collapse → hypoxaemia and respiratory failure. Treatment: exogenous surfactant.
  • ARDS: Surfactant dysfunction → alveolar collapse, atelectasis, and shunt. Treatment: lung-protective ventilation, PEEP, recruitment manoeuvres, prone positioning.
  • PEEP: Positive end-expiratory pressure stents open alveoli, reducing the pressure gradient that promotes collapse.
  • Alveolar recruitment: Sustained inflation or high PEEP recruits collapsed alveoli — once open, they are easier to keep open.
  • Bubble CPAP: Used in neonates — creates oscillating pressure that helps maintain alveolar recruitment.
  • Surfactant replacement therapy: Administered via ETT in neonates; investigational in adults with ARDS.
Laplace's Law & Alveolar Collapse In ARDS, surfactant dysfunction and alveolar oedema increase surface tension. Small alveoli collapse (higher pressure), leading to shunt and hypoxaemia. PEEP works by increasing alveolar pressure, counteracting the tendency to collapse. This is Laplace's law in clinical action.

Laplace's Law in Other Clinical Contexts

  • Aneurysms: As an aneurysm enlarges, wall tension increases (Law of Laplace: T = P × r). This promotes further dilation and rupture.
  • Ventricular dilation: In heart failure, a dilated ventricle has increased wall tension, increasing myocardial oxygen demand — a vicious cycle.
  • Bladder distension: As the bladder fills, wall tension increases — stimulating stretch receptors.
  • Endotracheal tube cuffs: Cuff pressure and radius determine wall tension — important for preventing tracheal mucosal ischaemia.
  • Gastrointestinal distension: Bowel dilation increases wall tension — a factor in ileus and obstruction.
⚠ Laplace's Law & ETT Cuffs Cuff pressure must be monitored to avoid exceeding capillary perfusion pressure (~25–30 cmH₂O). High cuff pressure → mucosal ischaemia → tracheal stenosis. The cuff radius also matters: overinflating a cuff increases wall tension, even if pressure seems acceptable. Use the minimal occlusive volume technique.
✦ Surfactant & Anaesthesia General anaesthesia reduces functional residual capacity (FRC) and promotes atelectasis. Surfactant dysfunction — from inflammation, oedema, or prolonged high FiO₂ — worsens this. Strategies to preserve surfactant function include: avoiding high FiO₂, using PEEP, minimising volutrauma, and maintaining normothermia.

Solubility & Inhaled Anaesthetic Uptake

  • Low blood–gas solubility (desflurane, sevoflurane): Alveolar partial pressure rises quickly → fast induction and recovery. The anaesthetic is carried in the blood but not tightly bound — it equilibrates rapidly.
  • High blood–gas solubility (halothane): Large amounts dissolve in blood before alveolar partial pressure rises → slow induction and recovery.
  • The concentration effect: High inspired concentrations increase the rate of alveolar partial pressure rise — accelerating induction.
  • The second gas effect: N₂O uptake concentrates the volatile agent in the alveoli — increasing its partial pressure and accelerating uptake.
✦ Choosing an Agent For rapid induction and recovery (day surgery): desflurane or sevoflurane. For maintenance with stable haemodynamics: isoflurane or sevoflurane. For low-flow anaesthesia: sevoflurane or isoflurane. Halothane is now rarely used in developed countries but remains relevant in resource-limited settings.

Module 08 of 10
Equipment Applications From Cylinders to Ventilators
How gas laws govern the design and function of anaesthetic equipment — cylinders, flowmeters, vaporisers, ventilators, and circuits.

Gas Cylinders

  • Boyle's law: Cylinder pressure × cylinder volume = constant (at constant temperature). As gas is withdrawn, pressure falls.
  • Gay-Lussac's law: Cylinder pressure rises with temperature — never store above 50°C.
  • Oxygen: Stored as compressed gas (200 bar in a full cylinder). The cylinder contains ~3,000 L of oxygen at STP.
  • Nitrous oxide: Stored as a liquid (at ~50 bar at 20°C). As gas is withdrawn, liquid vaporises, maintaining pressure until the liquid is exhausted. The cylinder weight is a more reliable indicator of remaining content than pressure.
  • Entonox (50% O₂ / 50% N₂O): Stored as a gas mixture. At low temperatures, the N₂O can separate out (the "Poynting effect") — warming the cylinder before use is essential.
  • Air: Compressed gas (200 bar) — used for ventilators and as a diluent for oxygen.
The Poynting Effect In Entonox, N₂O (more soluble) can dissolve in the liquid O₂ phase at low temperatures, altering the delivered mixture. This is why Entonox cylinders must be stored above 5°C and inverted before use if they have been in the cold.

Flowmeters (Rotameters)

  • Principle: A tapered tube with a float. Gas flows upward, lifting the float. The height of the float indicates flow rate.
  • Bernoulli's principle: Gas velocity increases as it passes the float, reducing pressure above the float and lifting it further.
  • Calibration: Flowmeters are calibrated for specific gases — a helium flowmeter reads differently from an oxygen flowmeter due to differences in viscosity and density.
  • Laminar vs. turbulent flow: At low flows, the flowmeter operates in the laminar range; at high flows, turbulent flow may develop, affecting accuracy.
  • Positioning: Flowmeters must be vertical for accurate readings.

Vaporisers

  • Variable bypass vaporiser: Splits fresh gas into a carrier stream that passes through the vaporiser chamber and a bypass stream that does not. The ratio is controlled by a dial.
  • Temperature compensation: Vaporisers use bimetallic strips or expansion elements to compensate for temperature changes — because vaporiser output increases with temperature (Gay-Lussac's law).
  • Dalton's law: The vapour pressure of the agent determines the concentration delivered. At 20°C, sevoflurane has a vapour pressure of 160 mmHg — enough to deliver clinical concentrations.
  • Henry's law: The amount of vapour produced depends on the solubility of the agent in the carrier gas — which is low for volatile agents.
  • Back-pressure effects: Positive pressure ventilation can affect vaporiser output — modern vaporisers are designed to minimise this.
  • Filling: Keyed fillers prevent wrong-agent filling. Agent-specific vaporisers are colour-coded.
⚠ Vaporiser Safety Never tilt a vaporiser — liquid anaesthetic can enter the bypass stream, delivering a dangerously high concentration. Always turn off the vaporiser and ensure it is upright before transport. Check the vaporiser is locked before use.

Ventilators

  • Boyle's law: The bellows or piston compresses gas — reducing volume, increasing pressure — to deliver a tidal volume.
  • Charles's law: Gas compression generates heat — ventilators must handle the temperature rise.
  • Poiseuille's law: The resistance of the breathing circuit and ETT determines the pressure required to deliver flow. Smaller ETTs require higher driving pressures.
  • Laplace's law: PEEP is used to maintain alveolar recruitment — counteracting the tendency of small alveoli to collapse.
  • Flow sensors: Pneumotachographs measure flow by detecting the pressure drop across a fixed resistance — a direct application of Poiseuille's law.
  • Turbine ventilators: Use a high-speed turbine to generate flow — no compressed gas required.
  • ICU ventilators: Use a combination of valves, flow sensors, and feedback loops to deliver precise tidal volumes and pressures.

Circle System — The Physics of Rebreathing The circle system uses unidirectional valves to direct exhaled gas through a CO₂ absorber (soda lime) and back to the patient. Fresh gas flow can be as low as 0.5 L/min (closed circuit). The efficiency depends on the absorber, valves, and circuit volume. Heat and moisture are conserved — a major advantage.

Humidifiers & Heat Exchangers

  • Heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs): Capture heat and moisture from exhaled gas and return them to inhaled gas. They reduce the risk of hypothermia and airway drying.
  • Heated humidifiers: Actively warm and humidify gas — used for prolonged ventilation. Charles's law explains the volume increase as gas is warmed.
  • Nebuliser humidifiers: Use ultrasonic or jet nebulisation to create water droplets — Venturi effect and Bernoulli's principle.
  • Condensation: Water vapour condenses as gas cools in the expiratory limb — a common circuit hazard.
✦ The Anaesthetic Machine — A Physics Laboratory The anaesthetic machine integrates Boyle's law (cylinders), Charles's law (temperature effects), Dalton's law (gas mixing), Henry's law (vaporisation), Bernoulli's principle (flowmeters), and Poiseuille's law (circuit resistance). Understanding these laws is essential for safe practice and troubleshooting.

Clinical Integration Putting It All Together
How gas laws explain clinical phenomena — from altitude physiology to mechanical ventilation to hyperbaric therapy.

Altitude Physiology — Dalton & Henry in Action

  • Dalton's law: At altitude, barometric pressure falls. The fraction of oxygen remains 0.21, but the partial pressure falls — because Ptotal is lower.
  • Henry's law: At altitude, less gas is dissolved in blood because the partial pressure is lower.
  • Acclimatisation: Hyperventilation (via carotid body) → respiratory alkalosis → renal bicarbonate excretion. Increased 2,3-DPG → right shift → improved unloading. Increased erythropoietin → ↑Hb.
  • Clinical relevance: Patients with respiratory disease may need in-flight oxygen. Commercial aircraft cruise at ~8,000 ft cabin altitude — equivalent to 15% O₂ at sea level.

Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy — Dalton & Henry at High Pressure

  • Dalton's law: At 3 atm, the partial pressure of oxygen is tripled. Breathing 100% O₂ at 3 atm delivers a PaO₂ of ~2,000 mmHg.
  • Henry's law: The amount of dissolved oxygen in blood increases linearly with partial pressure. At 3 atm, dissolved O₂ alone can meet tissue demands — even without haemoglobin.
  • Clinical applications: Carbon monoxide poisoning (accelerates CO dissociation), decompression sickness (reduces bubble size), non-healing wounds (promotes angiogenesis), radiation injury.
  • Risks: Oxygen toxicity (seizures, pulmonary damage), barotrauma (ear, sinus, lung), fire risk (high O₂ environment).

Cardiopulmonary Bypass — Gas Exchange Without Lungs

  • Oxygenators: Use Henry's law — oxygen dissolves in blood across a membrane, driven by partial pressure gradients.
  • Dalton's law: The gas mixture (O₂, air, CO₂) is adjusted to achieve desired partial pressures.
  • Fick's law: Membrane thickness and surface area determine gas exchange efficiency.
  • Boyle's law: The roller pump or centrifugal pump compresses blood — pressure and volume changes are carefully controlled.
  • Hypothermia: Reduces metabolic demand (VO₂) — allows longer safe circulatory arrest times.

Air Embolism — Henry's Law in Reverse

  • Mechanism: Gas enters the circulation — from surgical field, IV lines, or decompression.
  • Henry's law: As pressure falls (e.g., during ascent), dissolved gas comes out of solution — forming bubbles.
  • Clinical effects: Bubbles obstruct capillaries, activate inflammation, and cause endothelial damage.
  • Treatment: 100% O₂, left lateral Trendelenburg position (to trap air in the right ventricle apex), hyperbaric therapy if severe.
  • Prevention: Avoid N₂O in at-risk patients; careful de-airing of lines; use of bubble detectors.

Pulse Oximetry — Light Absorption & Gas Laws

  • Beer-Lambert law: Light absorption is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing substance and the path length.
  • Oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin have different absorption spectra — the basis of pulse oximetry.
  • Henry's law: The amount of oxygen dissolved in blood is proportional to PaO₂ — but pulse oximetry measures saturation, not content.
  • Clinical pitfall: In CO poisoning, carboxyhaemoglobin absorbs light similarly to oxyhaemoglobin — SpO₂ reads falsely high. Co-oximetry is required.
✦ The Integrated Anaesthetist When you adjust the ventilator, you are applying Boyle's law. When you set the vaporiser, you are applying Dalton's and Henry's laws. When you choose an ETT, you are applying Poiseuille's law. When you use PEEP, you are applying Laplace's law. The gas laws are not abstract physics — they are the tools of your trade.
