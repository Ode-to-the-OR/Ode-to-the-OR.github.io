Anatomy of the Neuraxis
The vertebral column, the meninges, and the spaces between them. A sound anatomical model is the foundation of safe neuraxial access.

The Vertebral Column — Surface Landmarks

Identifying the correct level is the first step. The conus medullaris typically terminates at L1–L2 in adults, making the lumbar interspaces below L2 safe for spinal anaesthesia.

The Meninges and Neuraxial Spaces

The Epidural Space

  • Boundaries: Ligamentum flavum (posterior), dura mater (anterior), pedicles and intervertebral foramina (lateral)
  • Contents: Epidural fat, internal vertebral venous plexus (Batson's plexus), spinal nerve roots, lymphatics
  • Depth: Variable — typically 4–6 cm in the lumbar region in non-obese adults; deeper in obesity
  • Loss of resistance: The ligamentum flavum provides resistance to injection; entering the epidural space causes a sudden loss of resistance (LOR)
  • Segmental spread: Epidural anaesthesia spreads segmentally; the level of insertion determines the dermatomes affected
  • Negative pressure: The epidural space has a slightly negative pressure (especially in the thoracic region), which is the basis of the "hanging drop" technique

The Subarachnoid Space

  • Contents: Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), spinal cord (cervical to L1–L2), cauda equina (below L2), arachnoid trabeculae
  • CSF volume: ~150 mL total; ~30–40 mL in the lumbar subarachnoid space
  • CSF density: 1.000–1.007 g/mL at 37°C; hyperbaric solutions (with dextrose) sink; isobaric solutions remain at the injection level
  • CSF pressure: 5–15 cmH₂O (lumbar, lateral decubitus); higher in obesity, pregnancy, and raised ICP
  • Spinal needle endpoint: Free flow of CSF confirms subarachnoid placement
  • CSF composition: Clear, colourless; glucose 50–80 mg/dL (60% of plasma); protein 15–45 mg/dL; pH 7.33–7.35

Loss of Resistance (LOR) Technique

  • Saline LOR: Most common; continuous pressure on plunger; loss of resistance when entering epidural space
  • Air LOR: Less commonly used; risk of false LOR if air is compressed; may cause patchy block if air enters epidural space
  • Hanging drop: A drop of saline is placed on the needle hub; it is sucked in when the epidural space (negative pressure) is entered
  • Ultrasound guidance: Can identify the level, estimate depth to the epidural space, and visualise the needle path — especially useful in difficult anatomy
✦ Ultrasound for Neuraxial Ultrasound can visualise the ligamentum flavum, dura, and epidural space. It helps identify the correct intervertebral level (especially in obesity where Tuffier's line is unreliable), measure depth to the epidural space, and guide paramedian approaches. It does not replace tactile LOR but improves first-pass success.

Assessing Block Height

  • Pinprick: Use a short-bevel needle; test from caudad to cephalad; the level at which sharp sensation is lost is the sensory level
  • Cold sensation: Ethyl chloride spray or ice — tests temperature (C and Aδ fibres)
  • Light touch: Cotton wool — tests Aβ fibres (later to be blocked)
  • Motor block: Bromage scale (0–3) — assesses quadriceps strength
  • Documentation: Record the sensory level (bilateral), motor block, and any asymmetry

Bromage Scale — Motor Blockade

Module 03 of 09
Technique — Spinal, Epidural, CSE
Step-by-step technique for the three central neuraxial blocks — positioning, equipment, endpoints, and troubleshooting.

Spinal Anaesthesia — Step by Step

  1. Preparation: IV access, monitoring (ECG, SpO₂, NIBP), resuscitation equipment, vasopressors drawn up
  2. Position: Sitting or lateral decubitus; back flexed to open interspinous spaces
  3. Landmarks: Identify L3–L4 or L4–L5 (Tuffier's line); mark the site
  4. Aseptic technique: Chlorhexidine 2% in 70% alcohol; sterile drape; gloves; mask
  5. Local anaesthetic: 1% lidocaine skin wheal at the puncture site
  6. Needle insertion: 25–27G pencil-point needle; midline or paramedian; advance slowly
  7. Endpoint: Free flow of CSF — confirm by aspiration
  8. Injection: Hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% (2.5–3 mL for Caesarean; 2–3 mL for lower limb); inject slowly (10–15 seconds)
  9. Positioning: Immediately position patient (supine, sitting, lateral) to determine spread
  10. Assessment: Sensory level (pinprick), motor block (Bromage), haemodynamics

Epidural Anaesthesia — Step by Step

  1. Preparation: As for spinal; ensure epidural kit, catheter, filter, and test dose drugs are ready
  2. Position: Sitting or lateral; back flexed
  3. Landmarks: Identify level; mark; infiltrate with local anaesthetic
  4. Needle: 16–18G Tuohy needle; bevel directed cephalad
  5. Loss of resistance: Advance needle through ligaments; continuous pressure on plunger; LOR confirms epidural space
  6. Catheter: Thread 3–5 cm into epidural space; withdraw needle; secure catheter
  7. Aspiration: Check for blood or CSF — if positive, reposition
  8. Test dose: 3 mL lidocaine 1.5% with epinephrine 1:200,000; observe 3–5 minutes for HR rise or block
  9. Loading dose: 10–20 mL bupivacaine 0.25–0.5% (or ropivacaine) in incremental doses (3–5 mL at a time)
  10. Assessment: Sensory level, motor block, haemodynamics

Combined Spinal-Epidural (CSE) — Step by Step

  1. Epidural needle placement: As for standard epidural (LOR to epidural space)
  2. Spinal needle: Pass a 25–27G pencil-point spinal needle through the Tuohy needle until CSF is obtained
  3. Spinal injection: Inject the spinal dose (e.g., bupivacaine 2–2.5 mL + fentanyl 10–25 μg)
  4. Needle withdrawal: Withdraw spinal needle; thread epidural catheter 3–5 cm
  5. Test dose: As for epidural
  6. Assessment: Rapid onset (spinal component) + titratable duration (epidural catheter)

⚠ Never Inject Against Resistance If there is resistance to injection through an epidural catheter, do not force. It may be kinked, knotted, or intravascular. Withdraw slightly and reassess. Forcing can cause catheter rupture, intravascular injection, or epidural haematoma.
Module 04 of 09
Physiology of Neuraxial Blockade
Sympathetic blockade, sensory blockade, motor blockade — the physiological consequences of interrupting neural transmission at the spinal cord level.

Order of Blockade

Local anaesthetics block small, unmyelinated fibres first. This determines the sequence of clinical effects.

Fibre TypeFunctionBlock OrderClinical Effect
B fibres (preganglionic sympathetic)Vasomotor tone, sudomotor1stVasodilation, hypotension, warmth, dry skin
C fibres (pain, temperature)Nociception, temperature2ndLoss of pain and temperature sensation
Aδ fibres (pain, touch)Sharp pain, touch3rdLoss of sharp pain and crude touch
Aβ fibres (touch, pressure)Light touch, proprioception4thLoss of light touch and proprioception
Aα fibres (motor)Skeletal muscle5thMotor blockade (last to be affected)
The Sympathetic Level is Higher Than the Sensory Level Sympathetic blockade extends 2–6 dermatomes above the sensory level. This is why hypotension occurs even with a sensory level that seems "low" — the sympathectomy is more extensive than the sensory block.

Haemodynamic Effects

  • Hypotension: From sympathetic blockade → arterial and venous dilation → reduced SVR and preload. Incidence: 30–50% in spinal anaesthesia; higher in obstetrics and the elderly.
  • Bradycardia: From blockade of cardiac accelerator fibres (T1–T4) and reduced venous return (Bezold–Jarisch reflex). Risk is higher with high blocks, young age, and β-blockers.
  • Compensatory mechanisms: Upper body vasoconstriction (if block is below T4), increased heart rate (if cardiac sympathetics are spared), renin-angiotensin activation.
  • Management: Preload/co-load with crystalloid (limited evidence), vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine, ephedrine), left uterine displacement in obstetrics, Trendelenburg position.
⚠ Prevention of Spinal Hypotension Ondansetron/Granisetron (5-HT₃ antagonists) have been studied for prevention of spinal-induced hypotension in Caesarean delivery, with some evidence of reduced incidence and vasopressor requirement. However, vasopressors remain the mainstay. Phenylephrine infusion is first-line in obstetrics; norepinephrine is an alternative.

Respiratory Effects

  • Low blocks: Minimal effect on ventilation; intercostal muscles may be affected with high thoracic blocks
  • High blocks (T4 and above): Intercostal muscle paralysis → reduced FRC and vital capacity; diaphragm (C3–5) is usually spared
  • Apnoea: Rare; occurs with very high blocks (cervical) or when local anaesthetic spreads to the brainstem (total spinal)
  • Clinical note: Patients with severe respiratory disease may not tolerate high blocks; consider the risk-benefit ratio

Gastrointestinal Effects

  • Nausea and vomiting: From hypotension, vagal activation, and gastrointestinal hypomotility
  • Reduced bowel motility: Sympathetic blockade → unopposed parasympathetic activity → increased peristalsis (often beneficial)
  • Stress response attenuation: Neuraxial blocks reduce the surgical stress response (cortisol, catecholamines) — may improve outcomes in high-risk patients

Thermoregulatory Effects

  • Vasodilation below the block: Increased heat loss from vasodilated skin
  • Impaired shivering: Below the block, shivering is blocked; the patient cannot generate heat through shivering in the affected dermatomes
  • Redistribution: Core heat redistributes to the periphery
  • Result: Neuraxial anaesthesia is associated with hypothermia — active warming is required

Neuroendocrine Effects

  • Attenuation of surgical stress response: Reduced cortisol, catecholamines, ADH, and renin-angiotensin activation
  • Benefit: May reduce cardiac morbidity in high-risk patients (especially with thoracic epidural for abdominal/thoracic surgery)
  • Limitation: The benefit is less clear in modern era with multimodal analgesia and minimally invasive surgery

Baricity — Spinal Anaesthesia

  • Hyperbaric: Local anaesthetic mixed with dextrose (density > CSF). Sinks to the dependent portion of the subarachnoid space. Position determines spread.
  • Isobaric: Density ≈ CSF. Remains at the level of injection; minimal position effect.
  • Hypobaric: Density < CSF. Rises to the non-dependent portion; used for specific procedures (e.g., hip surgery in lateral position).
Hyperbaric Bupivacaine — The Workhorse Most spinal anaesthetics use 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine (5 mg/mL with 8% dextrose). The patient's position after injection determines the spread: sitting → saddle block; lateral → unilateral; Trendelenburg → high block.

Spinal Anaesthesia — Technique

  • Needle: 25–27G pencil-point (Whitacre, Sprotte) — reduces PDPH risk compared to cutting needles
  • Position: Sitting or lateral decubitus; hyperbaric solution → position determines spread
  • Endpoint: Free flow of CSF
  • Injection: Slow injection (over 10–15 seconds) to avoid turbulence; barbotage only if necessary
  • Level: L3–L4 or L4–L5 (below conus)
  • Common doses: Hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% — 2.5–3 mL (12.5–15 mg) for Caesarean; 2–3 mL for lower limb; 1–2 mL for perineal

Epidural Anaesthesia — Technique

  • Needle: 16–18G Tuohy needle (blunt, curved tip)
  • Endpoint: Loss of resistance (LOR) — saline or air
  • Catheter: Threaded 3–5 cm into the epidural space
  • Test dose: 3 mL lidocaine 1.5% with epinephrine 1:200,000 — to detect intravascular or intrathecal placement
  • Loading dose: 10–20 mL bupivacaine 0.25–0.5% (or ropivacaine) in incremental doses
  • Maintenance: Continuous infusion, PCEA, or programmed intermittent epidural boluses (PIEB)
⚠ The Test Dose A test dose of lidocaine 1.5% 3 mL with epinephrine 1:200,000 is given to detect:
• Intravascular placement: Heart rate increases >20 bpm within 1–2 minutes.
• Intrathecal placement: Rapid onset of sensory/motor block within 3–5 minutes.

The test dose is not 100% sensitive but remains standard practice.

Combined Spinal-Epidural (CSE)

  • Technique: Epidural needle placed, then a spinal needle passed through it (needle-through-needle) to inject the spinal dose; epidural catheter then threaded
  • Advantages: Rapid onset (spinal) + titratable duration (epidural catheter)
  • Indications: Labour analgesia, Caesarean delivery, difficult anatomy where epidural alone may fail
  • Dural puncture epidural (DPE): A modification where the dura is punctured but no spinal drug is injected — allows confirmation of epidural placement and may improve block quality
  • Note: CSE is associated with a higher incidence of pruritus (from intrathecal opioid) and foetal bradycardia in obstetrics
✦ Labour Analgesia — Current Best Practice Low-concentration local anaesthetic (bupivacaine 0.0625–0.125% or ropivacaine 0.1–0.2%) combined with a lipophilic opioid (fentanyl 2 μg/mL or sufentanil) provides excellent analgesia with minimal motor block. Maintenance with PCEA + PIEB improves analgesia quality and reduces clinician workload compared to continuous infusion alone.

Post-Dural Puncture Headache (PDPH)

  • Incidence: ~1% with fine pencil-point spinal needles; 0.5–1% with accidental dural puncture during epidural (higher with large-bore needles)
  • Mechanism: CSF leak through dural hole → intracranial hypotension → traction on pain-sensitive structures
  • Presentation: Postural headache (worse upright, relieved supine), onset within 24–48 hours, often frontal/occipital, may be associated with neck stiffness, photophobia, nausea
  • Diagnosis: Clinical; MRI may show intracranial hypotension (pachymeningeal enhancement, sagging brain)
  • Treatment: Conservative (hydration, caffeine, simple analgesia) → Epidural blood patch (EBP) — gold standard; 10–20 mL autologous blood into epidural space; effective in >70%
  • Newer monitoring: Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) ultrasound may predict EBP response; ΔONSD ≥0.40 mm associated with improvement
Epidural Blood Patch — The Gold Standard For persistent PDPH, EBP is the definitive treatment. Aseptic technique; 10–20 mL autologous blood drawn from the patient's arm; injected into the epidural space at or below the level of dural puncture. Success rate >70% after first patch; >90% after second.

⚠ Epidural Haematoma — Time is Neurological Function Epidural haematoma is a surgical emergency. If suspected, MRI immediately. Decompression within 6–8 hours offers the best chance of neurological recovery. Risk is increased with anticoagulation, traumatic puncture, and indwelling catheters during anticoagulation.

Post-Dural Puncture Headache — Prevention

  • Pencil-point needles: 25–27G Whitacre or Sprotte — significantly reduce PDPH compared to cutting (Quincke) needles
  • Orientation of needle bevel: Parallel to dural fibres (for cutting needles) — reduces CSF leak
  • Minimise dural punctures: Use pencil-point; avoid multiple attempts
  • Epidural blood patch at time of dural puncture: Not routinely recommended; consider if high-risk (large-bore needle, young, obstetric)
  • Conservative measures: Hydration, caffeine, bed rest (limited evidence)

Management of Total Spinal

  • Recognition: Rapid onset of profound hypotension, bradycardia, apnoea, loss of consciousness
  • Airway: Intubate and ventilate with 100% O₂
  • Circulation: Vasopressors (epinephrine, phenylephrine), IV fluids, CPR if cardiac arrest
  • Position: Trendelenburg (may limit further spread, but evidence limited)
  • Reassurance: The block will wear off — supportive care until recovery
  • Documentation: Incident report; review technique; consider alternative for future

Anticoagulation & Neuraxial Safety
The ASRA and ESAIC/ESRA guidelines — the evidence-based framework for performing neuraxial blocks in patients on antithrombotic drugs.
⚠ The Core Principle Neuraxial bleeding occurs in a non-expansive space (the spinal canal). Even small haematomas can cause permanent paralysis. The risk is increased by anticoagulants, traumatic puncture, and indwelling catheters during anticoagulation. Strict adherence to time intervals is mandatory.

ASRA 5th Edition (2025) — Key Principles

  • Risk stratification: Consider patient factors (age, spinal pathology, coagulopathy), procedural factors (traumatic vs atraumatic, catheter vs single-shot), and drug factors (half-life, renal clearance, reversibility)
  • Time intervals: Specific intervals must be observed before and after neuraxial procedures — these vary by drug, dose, and renal function
  • Traumatic puncture: If a traumatic (bloody) puncture occurs, the interval before restarting anticoagulation should be extended, and the patient monitored closely
  • Catheter management: The same intervals apply to catheter removal as to insertion — do not remove a catheter during peak anticoagulant effect
  • Ultrasound guidance: Does NOT modify the time intervals — it reduces the number of attempts but not the bleeding risk once a haematoma forms

⚠ Renal Impairment For DOACs and LMWH, renal function determines the time interval. In patients with CrCl <50 mL/min, intervals should be extended. Always check the most recent eGFR and follow drug-specific guidance.

Traumatic Puncture — What to Do

  • Definition: Bloody tap or multiple attempts (>3) at neuraxial access
  • Action: Consider cancelling the procedure if anticoagulation is planned; if a catheter is in situ, remove it and observe neurologically
  • Documentation: Record the number of attempts, presence of blood, and any patient concerns
  • Monitoring: Neurological observations for 24 hours; educate the patient to report new back pain or weakness
  • Restarting anticoagulation: Delay until haemostasis is assured; consider imaging if symptoms develop

ESAIC/ESRA Guidelines (2022)

  • Joint European guidelines with similar principles to ASRA
  • Emphasise shared decision-making — consider the risk of bleeding vs the benefit of neuraxial technique
  • Provide specific intervals for a wider range of drugs (including newer anticoagulants)
  • Recommend ultrasound guidance to reduce traumatic puncture — but note it does not change the pharmacological time intervals
  • Advise that drug measurement (e.g., anti-Xa for LMWH) may guide timing in selected patients
