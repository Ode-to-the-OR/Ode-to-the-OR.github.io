A Brief History
From cross-circulation to the modern membrane oxygenator — the milestones that made open-heart surgery possible.
The Central Problem To operate on the inside of the heart, you must stop it. But a stopped heart cannot circulate blood or exchange gas. CPB was invented to solve both problems simultaneously — a mechanical heart (pump) and mechanical lungs (oxygenator).

✦ Historical Note Gibbon's first successful operation in 1953 was the culmination of over 20 years of animal research. His patient, Cecilia Bavolek, survived and lived for decades. The story is one of the great translational research achievements in medicine.

CPB vs ECMO — A Note

CPB and ECMO share the same core technology — a pump and an oxygenator. The differences are in configuration, duration, and anticoagulation intensity. CPB is designed for hours (intraoperative), ECMO for days to weeks (ICU). CPB typically uses high-dose heparin and a cardiotomy suction; ECMO uses lower-dose heparin and a more biocompatible circuit.

Prime Solution

  • Purpose: Fill the circuit and de-air it before connecting to the patient
  • Composition: Crystalloid (usually balanced salt solution), ± albumin, ± mannitol, ± heparin, ± sodium bicarbonate
  • Volume: 1.0–1.5 L in adults (varies with circuit size); mini-circuits use less (~500–700 mL)
  • Effect: Haemodilution — reduces haematocrit (target Hct 25–30% on CPB); improves flow characteristics but reduces oxygen-carrying capacity
  • Retrograde autologous prime (RAP): Uses patient's own blood to prime the circuit — reduces haemodilution and transfusion requirements
  • Miniaturised circuits: Reduce prime volume and surface area — less haemodilution, less inflammation
⚠ Haemodilution The prime volume dilutes the patient's blood. A 70 kg patient (5 L blood volume) with a 1.5 L prime will have a haematocrit reduced by ~23%. Target Hct on CPB is 25–30% — below 20% increases the risk of neurological injury and organ dysfunction. Monitor and transfuse if necessary.

Module 03 of 10 · Core
Physiology of Bypass
What happens to the body when the heart and lungs are bypassed — haemodynamics, coagulation, inflammation, and the end-organ effects.

Haemodynamic Changes on CPB

  • Non-pulsatile flow: The pump generates continuous, non-pulsatile flow — unlike the native heart. This reduces the pulsatile energy that helps maintain microcirculation, lymphatic flow, and baroreceptor function.
  • Reduced SVR: Haemodilution, hypothermia, and inflammatory mediators cause vasodilation → hypotension. Managed with vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine) and flow adjustment.
  • Altered autoregulation: Cerebral, renal, and mesenteric autoregulation are impaired — organ perfusion becomes more pressure-dependent.
  • Loss of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction: The lungs are bypassed — no longer participate in gas exchange or V/Q matching.
  • Venous return: Gravity drainage or vacuum-assisted venous drainage (VAVD) — must be carefully monitored to avoid air entrainment or excessive negative pressure.
  • Cardiac output: Zero — the heart is stopped (cross-clamped) or fibrillating. The pump is the sole source of flow.

The Inflammatory Response

  • Contact activation: Blood contacts the artificial surface → factor XII activation, complement, and platelets
  • Ischaemia-reperfusion: Aortic cross-clamp → ischaemia; clamp release → reperfusion → oxidative stress, cytokine release
  • Cardiotomy suction: Shed blood is activated by tissue factor, fat, and air — major contributor to inflammation
  • Endotoxin: Gut hypoperfusion → bacterial translocation → endotoxaemia
  • Result: Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) — fever, vasodilation, capillary leak, leukocytosis
  • Consequences: Post-perfusion syndrome, acute lung injury, renal dysfunction, coagulopathy
  • Mitigation: Heparin-bonded circuits, mini-circuits, leukocyte filtration, steroids (selected), aprotinin (now restricted), off-pump surgery

The "Post-Perfusion Syndrome" A constellation of findings after CPB: fever, vasodilation, capillary leak, leukocytosis, and organ dysfunction. It is a form of SIRS triggered by the extracorporeal circuit and ischaemia-reperfusion. Severity correlates with bypass duration and is a major cause of post-operative morbidity.

✦ Clinical Choice Alpha-stat is standard for adult cardiac surgery — it preserves cerebral autoregulation and avoids hyperaemia. pH-stat is sometimes used in paediatric cardiac surgery because it promotes more uniform brain cooling and may improve outcomes in complex congenital repairs.

Non-Pulsatile vs Pulsatile Flow

  • Non-pulsatile: Standard CPB — continuous flow. Simpler, less haemolysis, but may impair microcirculation and lymphatic return.
  • Pulsatile: Some systems generate a pulse (e.g., intra-aortic balloon pump or modified roller pump). May improve microcirculation and reduce SIRS, but evidence for improved clinical outcomes is weak.
  • Physiological difference: Pulsatility helps maintain endothelial function and nitric oxide release. Non-pulsatile flow may increase SVR and reduce organ perfusion.
  • Current practice: Most adult CPB is non-pulsatile. Pulsatile flow is used selectively (e.g., paediatric, prolonged bypass, renal protection).

Hypothermia & Organ Protection
Why we cool the body — the metabolic, neurological, and myocardial benefits of hypothermia during CPB, and the evidence behind it.
Core Concept Hypothermia reduces metabolic rate and oxygen consumption, providing organ protection during ischaemia. It is the single most important protective strategy in cardiac surgery — used universally in CPB, even when not strictly necessary for the procedure.

Temperature Targets

The Q10 Effect

Q10 Temperature Coefficient Q10 = (Rate at T+10°C) / (Rate at T) ≈ 2–3
For every 10°C reduction in temperature, metabolic rate falls by 50–70%. At 18–20°C, CMRO₂ is ~20–30% of normal.
  • Clinical implication: At 28°C, cerebral metabolic rate is ~50% of normal — allowing circulatory arrest for a limited time.
  • Caveat: The Q10 effect is not linear — it is greatest in the mild-to-moderate range and flattens at deep hypothermia.
  • Oxygen consumption: Falls from ~250 mL/min at 37°C to ~50 mL/min at 20°C — the basis of hypothermic protection.

Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA)

  • Definition: Deep hypothermia (18–20°C) with complete cessation of circulation — used for aortic arch surgery when the arch must be opened.
  • Safe duration: ~30–40 minutes at 18–20°C; longer durations require adjunctive cerebral perfusion.
  • Cerebral protection: Deep hypothermia reduces CMRO₂ to ~20% of baseline — allowing tolerance of ischaemia.
  • Adjuncts: Antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP), retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP), pH-stat strategy, barbiturates, steroids, topical cooling.
  • Rewarming: Slow rewarming (0.25–0.5°C/h) — rapid rewarming can cause cerebral hyperthermia, worsened neurological injury.
  • Complications: Neurological injury (stroke, cognitive dysfunction), coagulopathy, renal injury, spinal cord ischaemia.
⚠ The Danger of Rewarming Cerebral hyperthermia during rewarming (even mild, >37°C) is associated with worse neurological outcomes. Rewarm slowly, monitor core temperature (PA or oesophageal), and avoid overshoot. Target 36.5–37.0°C at the end of bypass.

Myocardial Protection — The Role of Hypothermia

  • Mechanism: Hypothermia reduces myocardial oxygen demand, preserving ATP and limiting ischaemic injury during aortic cross-clamping.
  • Cardioplegia: Hypothermic (4–8°C) or warm blood cardioplegia — delivered antegrade (aortic root) or retrograde (coronary sinus).
  • Topical cooling: Ice slush or cold saline applied to the heart surface — maintains local hypothermia.
  • Systemic hypothermia: Contributes to myocardial protection by reducing whole-body metabolic rate and oxygen demand.
  • Combined approach: Systemic hypothermia + cardioplegia + topical cooling — the standard of care.

Rewarming and Afterdrop

  • Rewarming rate: 0.25–0.5°C/h — slow to avoid cerebral hyperthermia and afterdrop.
  • Gradient: Keep the temperature gradient between blood and patient <10°C to avoid gas embolism and haemolysis.
  • Afterdrop: Continued fall in core temperature after rewarming begins — due to cold peripheral blood returning to the core.
  • Target: Core temperature 36.5–37.0°C at the end of CPB; avoid hyperthermia (>37.5°C).
  • Monitoring: Core temperature (PA, oesophageal, bladder) and skin temperature; watch for shivering during weaning.
✦ The Thermal Window Hypothermia protects by reducing metabolic demand, but it also impairs coagulation, immune function, and drug metabolism. The goal is to use the minimum degree of hypothermia needed for the procedure, then rewarm to normothermia. "Mild hypothermia for most cases, deep only when necessary."

Cardioplegia & Myocardial Protection
Stopping the heart safely — the composition, delivery, and evidence behind cardioplegic solutions. The cornerstone of myocardial protection.
The Problem During aortic cross-clamping, the heart is ischaemic. Without protection, the myocardium sustains irreversible injury within 20–40 minutes. Cardioplegia is the solution — it arrests the heart in diastole and protects it during ischaemia.

Principles of Cardioplegia

  • Rapid diastolic arrest: Stop the heart in diastole — reduces metabolic demand and avoids ischaemic contracture.
  • Hypothermia: Cool the myocardium (4–8°C) — reduces metabolic rate and oxygen demand.
  • Buffering: Maintain physiological pH — avoid intracellular acidosis.
  • Substrate enhancement: Provide glucose, glutamate, aspartate — fuel for ATP production.
  • Oxygenation: Blood cardioplegia delivers oxygen and nutrients — superior to crystalloid in many studies.
  • Repeated delivery: Re-dose every 15–20 minutes to maintain arrest and wash out metabolites.
  • Avoiding oedema: Hyperosmolar solutions (mannitol) reduce myocardial oedema.

Delivery Routes

  • Antegrade: Via aortic root (after cross-clamp) or directly into coronary ostia (after aortotomy). Preferred route — delivers solution to the myocardium through the coronary arteries.
  • Retrograde: Via the coronary sinus — delivers solution retrogradely through the venous system. Used when antegrade delivery is inadequate (aortic regurgitation, severe coronary disease, redo surgery).
  • Combined: Antegrade + retrograde — improves distribution to all myocardial territories.
  • Topical: Cold saline or ice slush applied to the heart surface — maintains local hypothermia.
  • Continuous vs intermittent: Continuous delivery maintains arrest but obscures the surgical field; intermittent (every 15–20 min) is standard.
⚠ Danger of Inadequate Delivery Incomplete cardioplegic delivery (e.g., in severe coronary stenosis, aortic regurgitation) results in inadequate myocardial protection and post-operative myocardial dysfunction. Use retrograde delivery, combined routes, or direct ostial cannulation to ensure complete protection.

Monitoring Myocardial Protection

  • ECG: Absence of electrical activity (asystole) — confirms arrest.
  • Myocardial temperature: Target <15°C — use a myocardial temperature probe.
  • Cardioplegia volume: Document volume delivered and effluent quality.
  • Coronary sinus effluent: Clear, not bloody — indicates adequate delivery and washout of metabolites.
  • Blood gases: Monitor acid-base and electrolytes during delivery.
  • Post-operative: Cardiac enzymes (troponin, CK-MB), ECG, echocardiography — assess for myocardial injury.

Reperfusion Injury

  • Mechanism: Restoration of blood flow to ischaemic myocardium → oxidative stress, calcium overload, inflammation, apoptosis.
  • Consequences: Myocardial stunning (reversible dysfunction), arrhythmias, cell death.
  • Mitigation: Controlled reperfusion (warm blood cardioplegia before cross-clamp release), antioxidants, ischemic preconditioning, postconditioning.
  • Warm reperfusion: Infuse warm blood cardioplegia (37°C) for 3–5 minutes before cross-clamp release — "hot shot" — improves recovery.
✦ The "Hot Shot" Before releasing the aortic cross-clamp, deliver a warm (37°C) blood cardioplegia solution — "hot shot" — to restore metabolic substrates, wash out metabolites, and reduce reperfusion injury. This is a standard practice in many centres and is associated with improved myocardial recovery.

Anticoagulation & Haemostasis
Heparin, protamine, and the coagulation cascade — preventing thrombosis during CPB while avoiding bleeding after it.
The Paradox CPB requires intense anticoagulation (high-dose heparin) to prevent clotting in the circuit — yet bleeding is a major complication after bypass. The balance between anticoagulation and haemostasis is the central haematological challenge of CPB.

Heparin — Mechanism and Monitoring

  • Mechanism: Binds to antithrombin III (ATIII) → conformational change → accelerates ATIII-mediated inactivation of thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa by 1000–10,000×.
  • Limitation: Heparin does not inhibit clot-bound thrombin or factor Xa bound to platelets — hence the need for high doses during CPB.
  • Dose for CPB: 300–400 IU/kg IV (typically 300 IU/kg) — target ACT >400–480 seconds.
  • Monitoring: Activated clotting time (ACT) — bedside test; target >400–480 s for CPB. Some centres use heparin concentration monitoring (Hepcon) for more precise dosing.
  • Heparin resistance: ATIII deficiency (congenital, consumptive), high doses needed — consider ATIII concentrate or fresh frozen plasma.
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT): Immune-mediated (anti-PF4 antibodies) → thrombosis risk. Alternative anticoagulants: argatroban, bivalirudin, danaparoid.

⚠ ACT is a Point-of-Care Test ACT measures the time to clot formation in whole blood — it is influenced by heparin, temperature, haemodilution, and platelet function. It is a useful bedside test but does not measure heparin concentration. For complex cases or heparin resistance, use heparin concentration monitoring (Hepcon) and anti-Xa levels.

Protamine — Reversal of Heparin

  • Mechanism: Positively charged protamine binds negatively charged heparin → inactive complex → removed by the reticuloendothelial system.
  • Dose: 1 mg protamine per 100 IU heparin (or based on total heparin dose). Give slowly (over 5–10 min) to avoid hypotension and anaphylaxis.
  • Monitoring: ACT after protamine — target within 10% of baseline. Repeat ACT 10–15 min after protamine to detect heparin rebound.
  • Side effects: Hypotension (histamine release, rapid injection), anaphylaxis (fish allergy, prior protamine exposure), pulmonary hypertension, coagulopathy (excess protamine is anticoagulant).
  • Heparin rebound: Heparin released from tissues hours after protamine — monitor ACT and bleeding; re-dose protamine if needed.
  • Protamine alternatives: Hexadimethrine (rarely used), heparinase (investigational).
⚠ Protamine Reaction Protamine can cause catastrophic anaphylaxis — especially in patients with prior protamine exposure (e.g., cardiac surgery, insulin users, vasectomy). Risk factors: fish allergy, NPH insulin, previous protamine reaction. Management: stop injection, epinephrine, fluids, vasopressors, and cardiopulmonary support if severe. Slow injection reduces the risk of histamine-mediated hypotension.

Antifibrinolytics — Tranexamic Acid

  • Mechanism: Lysine analogue — blocks plasminogen binding to fibrin → inhibits fibrinolysis.
  • Dose: 10–30 mg/kg IV (varies by centre); often given as a loading dose before CPB and/or added to pump prime.
  • Benefit: Reduces bleeding and transfusion requirements — strongest evidence for high-risk cases (redo, complex, dual antiplatelet therapy).
  • Risk: Theoretical risk of thrombosis; no clear evidence of increased thrombotic events in cardiac surgery. Avoid in patients with active thrombosis or severe renal impairment (dose adjust).
  • Aprotinin: Serine protease inhibitor — effective but associated with renal injury and mortality in the BART trial (2008); now restricted to selected high-risk cases.

Point-of-Care Coagulation Testing

  • TEG (Thromboelastography) / ROTEM (Rotational Thromboelastometry): Whole-blood viscoelastic tests — assess clot initiation, strength, and lysis in real time.
  • Parameters: R time (clot initiation), K time (clot kinetics), alpha angle (fibrin formation), MA (maximum amplitude — clot strength), LY30 (fibrinolysis).
  • Use: Guides targeted transfusion (platelets, FFP, cryoprecipitate, antifibrinolytics) — reduces unnecessary transfusion.
  • Limitations: Does not assess platelet function fully; requires trained operator; results take time.
✦ Blood Conservation Strategies Pre-operative: Correct anaemia (iron, erythropoietin), stop antiplatelet agents if safe, cell salvage. Intra-operative: Acute normovolaemic haemodilution (ANH), retrograde autologous prime (RAP), antifibrinolytics, meticulous surgical haemostasis, point-of-care testing, mini-circuits. Post-operative: Cell salvage, restrictive transfusion thresholds (Hb 7–8 g/dL), avoid hypothermia.

The Inflammatory Response
The systemic inflammatory response to CPB — mechanisms, consequences, and strategies to mitigate it. The hidden cost of extracorporeal circulation.
Historical Note The inflammatory response to CPB was first described in the 1980s as "post-perfusion syndrome" — a constellation of fever, vasodilation, capillary leak, and organ dysfunction. It remains a major cause of morbidity after cardiac surgery.

Mediators of the Inflammatory Response

  • Complement activation: C3a, C5a — anaphylatoxins; cause mast cell degranulation, neutrophil activation, capillary leak.
  • Cytokines: IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β — fever, vasodilation, capillary leak, acute phase response.
  • Neutrophil activation: Degranulation, ROS production, adhesion to endothelium — tissue injury.
  • Endothelial activation: Increased permeability, expression of adhesion molecules, nitric oxide dysregulation.
  • Coagulation activation: Tissue factor expression, thrombin generation — links inflammation and coagulation.
  • Fibrinolysis: tPA release — bleeding and inflammation.

The most effective strategies are minimising circuit surface area and prime volume, reducing cardiotomy suction, and shortening bypass duration. Anti-inflammatory drugs have shown disappointing results in large trials.

Other Complications

  • Gastrointestinal: Ileus, ischaemia, GI bleeding, pancreatitis — hypoperfusion and inflammation.
  • Infection: Sternal wound infection, mediastinitis, pneumonia, sepsis — immunosuppression, prolonged ICU stay.
  • Vascular: Aortic dissection (cannulation), limb ischaemia (femoral cannulation), emboli.
  • Metabolic: Hyperglycaemia, electrolyte disturbance, acidosis.
  • Psychological: Depression, PTSD, cognitive dysfunction.
⚠ Emergency Scenarios Massive air embolism: Trendelenburg, 100% O₂, aspirate air from aortic root, deep hypothermia, retrograde cerebral perfusion. Aortic dissection: Recognise by sudden malperfusion, hypotension, transoesophageal echo. Emergency repair. Circuit failure: Hand-crank the pump, replace oxygenator, call for help.

Weaning from Bypass
The transition from mechanical to native circulation — the criteria, the sequence, and the complications of weaning.
The Goal Safe separation from CPB requires adequate cardiac function, stable rhythm, optimised preload and afterload, and adequate gas exchange. Failure to wean is a major cause of morbidity and mortality.

Criteria for Weaning

  • Cardiac: Adequate contractility (visually assessed, echocardiography), stable rhythm (sinus or paced), no significant ischaemia.
  • Haemodynamic: Adequate preload (filling pressures, TEE), acceptable SVR, MAP >60 mmHg at reasonable flow rates.
  • Temperature: Core temperature ≥36.0°C (rewarmed).
  • Metabolic: Normal pH, electrolytes (K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺), glucose.
  • Coagulation: Heparin reversed (ACT within 10% of baseline), adequate platelets and clotting factors.
  • Respiratory: Lungs ventilated, adequate gas exchange.
  • Surgical: Haemostasis achieved, no mechanical issues (grafts, valves).

Step-by-Step Weaning

  1. Rewarm: Ensure core temperature ≥36°C. Slow rewarming (0.25–0.5°C/h) to avoid cerebral hyperthermia.
  2. Optimise preload: Assess filling pressures (CVP, PCWP, TEE). Transfuse if necessary (target Hct 25–30% on CPB, >30% after).
  3. Optimise rhythm: Treat arrhythmias (e.g., ventricular pacing for bradycardia, amiodarone for AF).
  4. Optimise electrolytes: K⁺ 4.0–5.0 mmol/L; Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ normal.
  5. Ventilate: Start mechanical ventilation; adjust FiO₂ (target SpO₂ >95%); PEEP 5 cmH₂O.
  6. Assess cardiac function: TEE — assess ventricular function, valve function, volume status.
  7. Reduce pump flow: Gradually reduce flow (10–20% steps) while monitoring haemodynamics (MAP, CVP, TEE).
  8. Assess native cardiac output: If MAP and cardiac function are adequate at low flow, proceed to separation.
  9. Separate: Clamp venous line, allow heart to fill, then clamp arterial line and decannulate.
  10. Protamine: Administer protamine to reverse heparin; monitor ACT.

Mechanical Circulatory Support

  • Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP): Counterpulsation — reduces afterload and improves coronary perfusion. First-line for low cardiac output after CPB.
  • Inotropes: Dobutamine (β₁), milrinone (PDE inhibitor), epinephrine (α/β), levosimendan (calcium sensitiser).
  • Vasopressors: Norepinephrine, vasopressin, phenylephrine.
  • ECMO: VA-ECMO for refractory cardiogenic shock after cardiac surgery (ECPR).
  • Ventricular assist devices (VAD): For refractory heart failure — bridge to recovery or transplant.
✦ The "Wean Trial" Before separating from CPB, perform a "wean trial" — reduce flow to 1–1.5 L/min/m² and assess haemodynamics, TEE, and cardiac output. If the patient tolerates the trial, separate. If not, identify the cause and treat.
