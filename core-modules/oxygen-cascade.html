Module 01 of 08
The Oxygen Cascade Step by Step
Oxygen moves passively down a partial-pressure gradient from dry atmospheric air to its point of use in the mitochondria. At each step, the PO₂ falls.
Definition The oxygen cascade describes the sequential fall in partial pressure of oxygen (PO₂) as it travels from the atmosphere → humidified tracheal gas → alveolar gas → arterial blood → systemic capillaries → tissues → mitochondria.

The Six Steps

Step 1 — Dry Atmospheric Gas

Atmospheric PO₂ PatmO₂ = PB × FIO₂
At sea level: 760 mmHg × 0.21 = 160 mmHg

Dry air at sea level contains 20.9% oxygen. Barometric pressure determines the partial pressure. At altitude, PB falls — the fraction remains 0.21 but the partial pressure falls proportionally.

Step 2 — Humidified Tracheal Gas

Humidified PO₂ PIO₂ = (PB − PH₂O) × FIO₂
PH₂O at 37°C = 47 mmHg · (760 − 47) × 0.21 = 149 mmHg

Inspired gas is fully humidified at body temperature by the time it reaches the trachea. This "dilutes" the oxygen partial pressure by approximately 11 mmHg.

Step 3 — Alveolar Gas

Ideal Alveolar Gas Equation PAO₂ = PIO₂ − (PaCO₂ / R)
R (respiratory quotient) ≈ 0.8 for mixed diet · Normal PAO₂ ≈ 100–104 mmHg

Alveolar oxygen is determined by:

  • PIO₂ — inspired partial pressure (affected by FiO₂ and barometric pressure)
  • Alveolar ventilation — inversely related: PAO₂ ∝ 1 / V̇A
  • CO₂ production and elimination — PaCO₂ in the numerator; a rise in PaCO₂ lowers PAO₂

Step 4 — Arterial Blood

The difference between alveolar and arterial PO₂ is the A-a gradient. Normal arterial PO₂ at sea level: 75–100 mmHg (declining with age).

The A-a gradient has three sources (1) Shunt — bronchial and thebesian veins drain deoxygenated blood directly into the left atrium (2–5% of cardiac output). (2) V/Q scatter — even in health, V/Q ratios vary across the lung. (3) Diffusion limitation — normally minimal at rest, but manifests during exercise or at altitude.

Step 5 — Systemic Capillary / Tissue

Oxygen diffuses from arterial blood to the tissue across the capillary wall. The Krogh cylinder model describes the radius of tissue supplied by a single capillary. Mixed venous PO₂ (Pv̄O₂) = 38–42 mmHg.

Step 6 — Mitochondria

Mitochondrial PO₂ is typically 1–10 mmHg, varying by tissue and metabolic activity.

  • Pasteur point: ~1 mmHg — below this, oxidative phosphorylation ceases and anaerobic metabolism supervenes
  • Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the respiratory chain, forming H₂O
  • Incomplete reduction generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), normally scavenged by antioxidants

Oxygen Content, Delivery & Extraction
Partial pressure drives saturation. Saturation determines content. Content × cardiac output = delivery. Delivery ÷ consumption = extraction.

Key Insight At PaO₂ 100 mmHg with normal Hb and SaO₂ 97%: bound O₂ ≈ 19.5 mL/dL, dissolved O₂ ≈ 0.3 mL/dL. Dissolved oxygen contributes only ~1.5% of total content. This is why increasing PaO₂ above 100 mmHg adds almost nothing to content — but increasing Hb or SaO₂ adds substantially.

Oxygen Delivery (DO₂)

Global Oxygen Delivery DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10
CO in L/min · CaO₂ in mL/dL · ×10 converts dL to L · Normal: 520–720 mL/min/m² (or ~1000 mL/min)

DO₂ is the product of cardiac output and arterial oxygen content. A fall in either can compromise tissue oxygenation. The body compensates by increasing extraction or increasing cardiac output.

The DO₂–VO₂ Relationship At rest, DO₂ is approximately 4× VO₂ — a substantial reserve. As DO₂ falls, extraction increases to maintain VO₂. Below a critical threshold (DO₂crit), VO₂ becomes supply-dependent and anaerobic metabolism begins (lactate rises).

Oxygen Consumption (VO₂)

Fick Principle VO₂ = CO × (CaO₂ − Cv̄O₂) × 10
Normal: 100–180 mL/min/m² (~250 mL/min whole body) · Increases with fever, shivering, sepsis, exercise

Oxygen Extraction Ratio (O₂ER)

Extraction Ratio O₂ER = VO₂ / DO₂ × 100 = (CaO₂ − Cv̄O₂) / CaO₂ × 100
Normal: 22–30% · Maximum: ~70–80% (critical extraction threshold)
  • Normal: 20–30% of delivered oxygen is extracted at rest
  • Increased extraction: low CO, anaemia, hypoxaemia — tissues compensate by extracting more
  • Decreased extraction: sepsis, cyanide toxicity — cellular inability to utilise oxygen despite adequate delivery
  • ScvO₂ is a mirror of extraction: ↓ScvO₂ = ↑extraction; ↑ScvO₂ (with high lactate) = extraction failure
⚠ The Critical DO₂ Threshold When DO₂ falls below approximately 330 mL/min/m², VO₂ becomes supply-dependent — tissues can no longer extract enough oxygen. Lactate rises. This is the physiological basis of goal-directed therapy targeting DO₂ >600 mL/min/m² in high-risk surgical patients.

Worked Example — Putting It All Together

Case: A 70 kg patient in septic shock. Hb 9 g/dL, SaO₂ 96%, PaO₂ 85 mmHg, CO 7.5 L/min. Lactate 4.2 mmol/L. ScvO₂ 78%.

Step 1 — CaO₂: (1.34 × 9 × 0.96) + (0.003 × 85) = 11.6 + 0.26 = 11.9 mL/dL (low — anaemia)
Step 2 — DO₂: 7.5 × 11.9 × 10 = 892 mL/min (or ~509 mL/min/m² — low)
Step 3 — VO₂: assume normal ~250 mL/min (elevated in sepsis)
Step 4 — O₂ER: 250/892 × 100 = 28% (appears normal)
Step 5 — Interpretation: Despite normal-looking extraction, ScvO₂ 78% with lactate 4.2 suggests microvascular shunting / extraction failure. The problem is not delivery alone — it is distribution and cellular utilisation. Target: transfuse to Hb 9–10 g/dL, optimise CO, consider inotrope if CI <2.5, treat source.

The Oxyhaemoglobin Dissociation Curve
The sigmoid relationship between PaO₂ and SaO₂ — the single most important graph in oxygen physiology.

The Sigmoid Shape — Why It Matters

  • Upper flat portion (PaO₂ >60 mmHg): Large changes in PaO₂ produce small changes in SaO₂. This provides a safety margin — even if PaO₂ falls from 100 to 70, SaO₂ remains >90%.
  • Steep portion (PaO₂ 20–60 mmHg): Small changes in PaO₂ produce large changes in SaO₂. This is where oxygen unloading occurs in tissues.
  • PaO₂ 60 mmHg = the critical inflection point: SaO₂ ~90%. Below this, saturation falls precipitously.
The Clinical Target: PaO₂ >60 mmHg Above 60 mmHg, the curve is flat — increasing PaO₂ adds little to saturation or content. Below 60 mmHg, the curve is steep — small further falls cause rapid desaturation. This is why 60 mmHg is the therapeutic threshold.

P50 — The Position Marker

Definition P50 = the PaO₂ at which haemoglobin is 50% saturated
Normal P50 = 27 mmHg (range 24–28) · ↑P50 = right shift · ↓P50 = left shift

⚠ The Bohr Effect Active tissues produce CO₂, H⁺, and heat. These shift the curve right, decreasing haemoglobin's affinity for O₂ and promoting unloading exactly where it's needed most. This is physiological elegance — the tissues signal their demand by altering the local environment.
✦ 2,3-DPG in Stored Blood Banked blood loses 2,3-DPG over 1–2 weeks → left shift → impaired oxygen unloading. Massive transfusion can paradoxically reduce tissue oxygenation despite improving Hb. 2,3-DPG regenerates within 6–24 hours in vivo.

The Hill Equation

Hill Equation SaO₂ = Pn / (Pn + P50n)
n = Hill coefficient ≈ 2.8 for human Hb · describes positive cooperativity of O₂ binding

The sigmoid shape arises from cooperative binding: each O₂ molecule bound increases the affinity for the next. This is why the curve is not hyperbolic (like myoglobin) but sigmoid — and why it is so efficient at both loading in the lung and unloading in the tissues.

Mechanisms of Hypoxaemia
Five mechanisms — four common, one rare. Each has a characteristic A-a gradient and oxygen response.
Hypoxaemia vs Hypoxia Hypoxaemia = low PaO₂ or SaO₂ in arterial blood. Hypoxia = inadequate oxygen at the tissue level, which may occur with normal PaO₂ (e.g., anaemia, circulatory failure, cyanide toxicity).

The Five Mechanisms

MechanismA-a GradientPaCO₂O₂ ResponseClassic Causes
1. V/Q Mismatch (most common)↑N/↓GoodAsthma, COPD, PE, pneumonia
2. HypoventilationNormal↑GoodOpioids, obesity, neuromuscular
3. Shunt↑N/↓Poor (>30% shunt)ARDS, atelectasis, pneumonia, intracardiac
4. Diffusion Limitation↑N/↓GoodInterstitial lung disease, exercise at altitude
5. Low Inspired PO₂Normal↓ (hyperventilation)GoodAltitude, anaesthetic circuit failure

Mechanism 1 — V/Q Mismatch

Regional differences in ventilation and perfusion create lung units with high V/Q (dead space) and low V/Q (shunt-like). Low V/Q units contribute most to hypoxaemia because they are perfused but poorly ventilated.

  • Normal V/Q: ~0.8 overall (4 L/min ventilation / 5 L/min perfusion)
  • Low V/Q (→0): shunt-like — blood passes poorly ventilated alveoli
  • High V/Q (→∞): dead space — ventilated but not perfused (PE, emphysema)
  • Response to O₂: Good — high FiO₂ converts low V/Q units to higher PAO₂

Mechanism 2 — Hypoventilation

Alveolar Gas Equation Rearranged ↓V̇A → ↑PaCO₂ → ↓PAO₂
For every 1 mmHg rise in PaCO₂, PAO₂ falls by ~1.25 mmHg (assuming R = 0.8)

The A-a gradient is normal — the problem is not gas exchange across the membrane but inadequate delivery of fresh gas to the alveoli.

Mechanism 3 — Shunt

Shunt Fraction Q̇s/Q̇t = (CcO₂ − CaO₂) / (CcO₂ − Cv̄O₂)
CcO₂ = end-capillary content (calculated) · Normal shunt <5%
  • Blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely — no gas exchange occurs
  • Does not respond to supplemental O₂ when shunt >30%: the shunted blood never sees the increased FiO₂
  • Two types: (1) Anatomical — intracardiac (ASD, VSD), pulmonary AV malformations; (2) Physiological — atelectasis, consolidation, ARDS
  • Treatment: recruit the shunt (PEEP, positioning, secretion clearance) or reduce shunt fraction (prone positioning)

Mechanism 4 — Diffusion Limitation

  • Alveolar–capillary membrane thickening (interstitial fibrosis, oedema) or shortened transit time (exercise, high output)
  • Normal transit time: ~0.75 seconds at rest — equilibration complete by 0.25 seconds (3× reserve)
  • During exercise: transit time falls to ~0.25 seconds — reserve eliminated
  • At altitude: reduced driving pressure compounds the problem
  • Responds well to O₂ — increased PAO₂ restores the diffusion gradient

Mechanism 5 — Low Inspired PO₂

  • Altitude: PB falls → PIO₂ falls (fraction unchanged)
  • Anaesthetic circuit failure: hypoxic gas mixture, disconnected O₂ supply
  • Response: Normal A-a gradient; responds to increased FiO₂ (if available)
✦ Anaesthesia-Specific Causes Atelectasis (most common): from supine position, muscle relaxation, PPV, O₂ resorption. Diffusion hypoxia: N₂O washout after cessation dilutes alveolar O₂ — give 100% O₂ for 5–10 minutes. Reduced FRC: from anaesthesia, obesity, Trendelenburg, pneumoperitoneum.

Quick Reference — Response to O₂

Clinical Scenarios

Patient: 65-year-old, BMI 38, 4 hours post-laparotomy. SpO₂ 89% on 4L nasal cannula. RR 22, shallow. PaCO₂ 52 mmHg (baseline 40).

Analysis: A-a gradient = elevated (calculate: PAO₂ = 149 − 52/0.8 = 84; PaO₂ ~55 → A-a = 29). Elevated A-a + elevated PaCO₂ = mixed hypoventilation + V/Q mismatch + atelectasis. The post-operative triad: residual anaesthetic → hypoventilation → atelectasis → shunt.

Management: Sit up, incentive spirometry, CPAP/HFNC, escalate analgesia, consider reversal agents if applicable. Avoid excessive FiO₂ which worsens resorption atelectasis.
Patient: 45-year-old with severe pancreatitis. PaO₂ 58 on FiO₂ 1.0, PEEP 15. Bilateral infiltrates. P/F ratio = 58.

Analysis: Severe shunt — consolidation, oedema, and derecruitment create true shunt physiology. Not responding to high FiO₂ confirms shunt >30%.

Management: Lung-protective ventilation (Vt 6 mL/kg IBW, Pplat <30), PEEP titration, prone positioning, neuromuscular blockade if P/F <150, ECMO if P/F <80 despite optimisation.
Patient: 30-year-old post-op, PCA morphine. RR 6, SpO₂ 84% on room air. PaCO₂ 68 mmHg.

Analysis: PaO₂ ~45, A-a gradient = 149 − 85 = 64... but wait, at PaCO₂ 68, PAO₂ = 149 − 68/0.8 = 64. PaO₂ 45 → A-a = 19 → normal for age. Pure hypoventilation.

Management: Naloxone titrated, supplemental O₂ (responds well), reduce opioid dose, consider multimodal analgesia.

Types of Hypoxia
The classic Barcroft classification — four types plus the anaesthetic-specific fifth. Each has a distinct physiological signature.

The Classification

1 · Hypoxic (Hypoxaemic) Hypoxia

The arterial blood itself is poorly oxygenated — PaO₂ is low, and haemoglobin saturation falls accordingly. This is the classic "low oxygen in the blood" state.

  • Low inspired PO₂: altitude, anaesthetic circuit with low FiO₂
  • Alveolar hypoventilation: opioids, neuromuscular blockade, obesity
  • V/Q mismatch: COPD, asthma, pneumonia
  • Shunt: ARDS, atelectasis, congenital heart disease
  • Diffusion limitation: interstitial lung disease
Treatment Increase FiO₂. Treat the underlying cause (bronchodilators, recruitment, drainage). Shunt >30% will not respond well to O₂.

2 · Anaemic (Hypaemic) Hypoxia

PaO₂ and SaO₂ are normal, but the oxygen-carrying capacity is reduced — either because there is less haemoglobin or because the haemoglobin that exists cannot bind oxygen normally.

  • Quantity problem: haemorrhage, iron-deficiency anaemia, chronic kidney disease
  • Quality problem: carboxyhaemoglobin (CO binds Hb with 200× affinity), methaemoglobinaemia (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺), sulphhaemoglobinaemia
⚠ Pulse Oximetry Trap In CO poisoning, SpO₂ reads falsely high — standard pulse oximeters cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin. A patient with SpO₂ 98% may be profoundly hypoxic. Use co-oximetry if suspected.

3 · Stagnant (Circulatory) Hypoxia

PaO₂, SaO₂, and CaO₂ are all normal. The problem is blood flow — oxygen is delivered too slowly to meet metabolic demand.

  • Global: cardiogenic shock, hypovolaemic shock, cardiac arrest
  • Local: arterial occlusion, venous thrombosis, compartment syndrome, tourniquet
  • Signature: widened a-v O₂ difference (↑↑ extraction) — tissues pull more O₂ from each unit of blood
  • ScvO₂ falls — the hallmark of stagnant hypoxia
✦ Anaesthesia Relevance Positioning (lithotomy, prone, Trendelenburg) can impair venous return and create regional stagnant hypoxia. Tourniquet use creates a controlled local stagnant hypoxia. Always assess perfusion (CRT, mottling, urine output) alongside SpO₂.

4 · Histotoxic Hypoxia

The oxygen is delivered to the tissue, but the cells cannot use it. Cytochrome oxidase is poisoned — oxidative phosphorylation fails despite normal delivery.

  • Cyanide: binds cytochrome c oxidase — classic histotoxic poison. Antidote: hydroxocobalamin
  • Sepsis: mitochondrial dysfunction, "cytopathic hypoxia"
  • Alcohol, barbiturates: depress cellular respiration
  • Signature: narrow a-v O₂ difference — tissues cannot extract oxygen. ScvO₂ is high (often >80%) despite elevated lactate
⚠ The Paradox High ScvO₂ + high lactate = histotoxic hypoxia. The blood is well-oxygenated and returns to the heart still well-oxygenated — because the tissues couldn't use it. This is not reassuring; it is a sign of cellular failure.

5 · Fulminating Hypoxia

A rapidly induced, severe hypoxic hypoxia caused by inhalation of undiluted inert gases — nitrogen, methane, helium, or nitrous oxide — displacing oxygen from the alveoli.

Anaesthetic Context Administering N₂O without oxygen (e.g., during induction with a hypoxic mixture) can cause fulminating hypoxia within seconds. This is why 100% O₂ is always given before and after N₂O, and why inspired oxygen concentration is continuously monitored.

A-a Gradient & Venous Oximetry
The two most useful bedside tools for distinguishing mechanisms of hypoxaemia and assessing tissue oxygenation.

The Alveolar–Arterial (A-a) Oxygen Gradient

A-a Gradient P(A-a)O₂ = PAO₂ − PaO₂
PAO₂ = PIO₂ − (PaCO₂ / R) · Normal: 5–20 mmHg (increases with age)
Age-Adjusted Normal Expected A-a = (Age / 4) + 4
Age 20: ~9 mmHg · Age 40: ~14 mmHg · Age 60: ~19 mmHg · Age 80: ~24 mmHg

Interpreting the A-a Gradient

A-a GradientMechanismConditions
NormalNo gas exchange defectHypoventilation · Low inspired PO₂
ElevatedGas exchange defectV/Q mismatch · Shunt · Diffusion limitation
The Diagnostic Power A patient with hypoxaemia and a normal A-a gradient has a problem with ventilation or inspired gas — not with the lung's gas exchange surface. Check PaCO₂ (elevated = hypoventilation) and consider altitude or circuit failure.

A patient with hypoxaemia and an elevated A-a gradient has a problem with the lung itself — V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion limitation. The A-a gradient cannot distinguish among these three; clinical context and O₂ response are needed.

The P/F Ratio and S/F Ratio

P/F Ratio (Horowitz Index) P/F = PaO₂ / FiO₂
Normal >400 · Mild ARDS 200–300 · Moderate 100–200 · Severe <100 (Berlin criteria, on PEEP ≥5)
S/F Ratio S/F = SpO₂ / FiO₂
Normal >400 · <315 suggests ARDS · Useful when ABG unavailable
  • P/F ratio is the standard for ARDS severity classification (Berlin definition)
  • Requires ABG — cannot be calculated from SpO₂ alone
  • S/F ratio is a non-invasive surrogate; acceptable for trending and screening
  • ROX index: (SpO₂/FiO₂)/RR — predicts HFNC failure; >4.88 at 2h, 12h suggests success

The Pv-aCO₂ Gap

Venoarterial CO₂ Difference Pv-aCO₂ = PvCO₂ − PaCO₂
Normal: 2–6 mmHg · >6 mmHg = inadequate CO₂ washout (low flow)
  • Elevated Pv-aCO₂ indicates inadequate cardiac output relative to metabolic demand
  • Valuable when ScvO₂ appears normalised but lactate remains high — "cryptic shock"
  • Pv-aCO₂ / Ca-vO₂ ratio >1.4 suggests anaerobic CO₂ production
  • Also useful to distinguish low-flow from cytopathic causes of high lactate
✦ The Integrated Assessment Low ScvO₂ + high lactate: low delivery (stagnant or hypoxic hypoxia) → increase CO, Hb, or SaO₂.

High ScvO₂ + high lactate: extraction failure (histotoxic or septic) → source control, mitochondrial support — not more oxygen.

Pulse Oximetry — Principles & Pitfalls

  • Based on differential absorption of red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm) light by oxy- and deoxyhaemoglobin
  • Pitfalls: CO poisoning (SpO₂ falsely high), methaemoglobinaemia (SpO₂ ~85% regardless), poor perfusion, motion, nail polish, intravenous dyes, severe anaemia (SpO₂ may be normal despite low CaO₂)
  • Accuracy: ±2–3% within 70–100% range; less accurate below 70%
  • PaO₂ vs SaO₂: Above PaO₂ 60 mmHg, changes in PaO₂ produce minimal change in SaO₂ — pulse oximetry is a poor detector of hyperoxia but excellent for hypoxaemia
⚠ Oxygen Toxicity Prolonged high FiO₂ (>0.6 for >24–48h) causes: absorption atelectasis, tracheobronchitis, diffuse alveolar damage, retinopathy of prematurity in neonates. Target SpO₂ 92–96% (88–92% in COPD) — avoid unnecessary hyperoxia.

Special Situations

One-Lung Ventilation (OLV)

  • Hypoxaemia occurs in ~5–10% of cases — due to shunt through the non-ventilated lung
  • Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV): compensatory mechanism redirecting blood to the ventilated lung; inhibited by volatile agents, vasodilators, and alkalosis
  • Management of OLV hypoxaemia: check tube position, suction, ensure FiO₂ 1.0, apply CPAP to non-dependent lung, PEEP to dependent lung, intermittent reinflation of non-ventilated lung, clamp pulmonary artery (rare)

Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB)

  • Whole-body inflammatory response → capillary leak → interstitial oedema → diffusion limitation
  • Atelectasis common post-bypass — from cardioplegia, retraction, CPB itself
  • Post-CPB hypoxaemia: shunt from atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, reduced FRC, diaphragmatic dysfunction
  • Management: recruitment manoeuvres, PEEP titration, early extubation protocols, physiotherapy

Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA)

  • Chronic intermittent hypoxaemia → oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction
  • Peri-operative risk: difficult airway, sensitivity to opioids/sedatives, post-operative hypoxaemia
  • Management: CPAP peri-operatively, regional techniques where possible, cautious opioid titration, monitoring in high-dependency setting

High-Altitude & Aerospace Physiology

  • Commercial aircraft cruise at 30,000–40,000 ft — cabin altitude ~6000–8000 ft (PB ~560–640 mmHg)
  • Healthy passengers: SpO₂ ~90–94% — usually asymptomatic
  • At-risk: COPD (may need in-flight O₂), recent pneumothorax, decompression sickness susceptibility, pregnancy >36 weeks
  • Supplemental O₂ recommended if PaO₂ <70 mmHg at sea level or SpO₂ <92% predicted

✦ The Anaesthetist's Rule Give oxygen for a reason, not by habit. Target SpO₂ 92–96% in most patients, 88–92% in COPD. Wean FiO₂ as soon as feasible. Avoid both hypoxaemia and hyperoxia — both cause harm.

Emerging & Advanced Concepts

  • Mitochondrial oxygen tension (mitoPO₂): measured by protoporphyrin IX fluorescence — direct assessment of cellular oxygenation; research tool
  • Sublingual microcirculation (SDF/IDF): direct visualisation of capillary flow — research and increasingly clinical
  • Artificial oxygen carriers: haemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs) and perfluorocarbons — investigational
  • Hyperbaric oxygen therapy: for CO poisoning, decompression sickness, non-healing wounds, radiation injury
  • ECMO oxygenation: VV-ECMO provides gas exchange independent of native lung; target PaO₂ 60–80, SaO₂ 85–92% to avoid hyperoxia
  • Personalised PEEP: electrical impedance tomography (EIT) and pressure–volume curve analysis to optimise recruitment
